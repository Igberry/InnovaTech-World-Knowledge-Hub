**Goal:** By the end of this short session you'll have a professional email, the core accounts, a corner
you can take a video call from, and a written power and data plan. Almost no reading - you set each thing
up as you go.

## What you'll be able to do

- Create and use a professional email and the core accounts (LinkedIn, a document tool)
- Set up and test a spot you can take a real video call from
- Write a backup plan for power and internet that sounds good in an interview
- Keep your job-search files tidy from day one

## Before you start (about 10 minutes)

- Have your phone or laptop and your usual internet ready.
- Open the [remote-readiness checklist](/downloads/remote-ready/remote-readiness-checklist.md) in a tab
  and keep it open all course. Tick items off as you finish them today.

## Try it 1 - Make a professional email

**Why it matters:** employers judge you on small signals before they read a word you wrote. A clean email
is a free, easy win.

**Do this now:**
1. Open Gmail or Outlook and create an address like `firstname.lastname@gmail.com`, not `coolguy2000@...`.
2. If your name is taken, add a middle initial or your field, e.g. `jane.e.okoro@gmail.com` or
   `jane.okoro.va@gmail.com`.
3. Send one test email to yourself to confirm it works.

**You've got it when:** you have a tidy email in your real name that you'd be happy to put on a CV.

## Try it 2 - Open the core accounts

**Why it matters:** you'll build on these every week. Get them created now so nothing blocks you later.

**Do this now:**
1. Create a **LinkedIn** account with the same name as your email (we build the profile properly in Week 2).
2. Set up a free **document tool**: Google Workspace (Docs, Sheets) or Microsoft 365 free.
3. In your document tool, make one blank doc called "My CV" so it's ready for Week 2.

**You've got it when:** your email, LinkedIn and document tool all use the same name, so you're easy to recognise.

## Try it 3 - Build and test your video-call corner

**Why it matters:** your first interview can be won or lost on sound and light before you say anything.

**Do this now:**
1. Pick a quiet spot with a plain wall behind you and a window in front of you (face the light).
2. Prop your phone or laptop so the camera sits near eye level, not looking up your nose.
3. Search "test my webcam" or open your video app's settings and check your camera and mic.
4. Record a 20-second clip of yourself saying hello. Watch it back. Fix the light, noise or angle.

**You've got it when:** you've watched a clip of yourself and it looks and sounds clear.

## Try it 4 - Write your power and data plan

**Why it matters:** NEPA and data drops are real, and interviewers will ask how you handle them. A calm,
specific plan is a strong answer.

**Do this now - write one line for each:**
1. **Power:** your charged power bank, and the nearest reliable place to work or charge.
2. **Data:** your backup SIM or data bundle, and a hotspot you trust.
3. **Communication:** the exact message you'd send if something failed, e.g. "My power is out, I'll be back
   by 4pm WAT."

**You've got it when:** you have a three-line plan you could read aloud in an interview without hesitating.

## Try it 5 - Set up your job-search folder

**Why it matters:** a tidy folder now saves hours of searching later and keeps versions straight.

**Do this now - create this folder structure:**

```
remote-ready/
├── cv/                (your CV versions, as PDFs)
├── applications/      (the application tracker, saved job adverts)
└── portfolio/         (your work samples)
```

**You've got it when:** the folder exists and your "My CV" doc lives inside `cv/`.

### Watch out for

- Treating the reliability plan as optional. It is often the thing that wins trust.
- A messy email or mismatched names across accounts.
- Skipping the camera and mic test and finding out mid-interview.

## This week's build: your ready-to-work setup

Finish the day with everything below done and ticked on the checklist:

1. A professional email in your real name.
2. LinkedIn and a document tool created, same name across all three.
3. A tested video-call corner (you've watched a clip of yourself).
4. A written three-line power and data plan.
5. The `remote-ready` folder set up.

## Mentoring Session Plan (90 minutes)

| Time | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0-10 min | Welcome; why reliability matters as much as skill in remote work |
| 10-35 min | Live: create emails and core accounts together; fix name mismatches |
| 35-60 min | Live: set up and test each learner's camera, mic, light and background |
| 60-80 min | Write and share power and data plans; practise the "something failed" message |
| 80-90 min | Set up the folder; brief Week 1 |

## Facilitator Notes

Many learners underestimate the "reliability" signal - stress that communication during power/data
outages is what builds trust, not pretending problems don't happen. Check that everyone's camera and
mic actually work in session one; a failed first interview over tech problems is demoralising and
avoidable. Keep personal details off public profiles.

## Can you do these now?

1. Why does a professional email address matter for remote work?
2. What makes a good spot for a video interview?
3. What is your plan if the power goes out during work hours?
4. Why is communicating about a problem often more important than the problem itself?
