**Goal:** Build a calm inbox and calendar routine, then keep practice files organised and safely shared.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this week, you can:

- Apply labels, filters and search operators to a fictional inbox.
- Create reusable replies and describe when a scheduled or delegated email needs extra care.
- Plan a weekly Calendar routine with a correct time zone and meeting invitation.
- Organise Drive files and explain the difference between restricted and link-based sharing.
- Use Contacts, Tasks and Keep as capture tools without storing unnecessary private data.
- Identify common phishing cues and review account security settings without sharing credentials.

## Tools / Accounts Needed This Week

A Google Account with access to Gmail, Calendar and Drive; a laptop browser is preferred. Use the fictional inbox file and a practice folder in an account you control. A school or work administrator may disable features. Do not switch to a paid plan for an assignment.

## Pre-Session Resources (about 2 hours)

- Read Google's [Gmail search operator guide](https://support.google.com/mail/answer/7190) (about 25 minutes). Test the examples in a practice inbox; do not search another person's messages.
- Read the Drive Help pages for [sharing files](https://support.google.com/drive/answer/2494822) and [shared drives](https://support.google.com/a/users/answer/7212025) (about 30 minutes). Shared drives are optional and depend on account and organisation settings.
- Read the official [2-Step Verification guide](https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/185839) (about 20 minutes).
- Search YouTube: **"Gmail labels filters templates beginner"** and **"Google Calendar time zone recurring event beginner"**. Choose recent tutorials and watch about 30 minutes total; interface steps change.
- Download the fictional inbox and folder template. Spend 15 minutes identifying what should be handled, scheduled, stored or ignored.

## Lessons

### Lesson 1: Make Gmail searchable and usable

An email inbox is a queue, not a filing cabinet. You do not need to keep every message at the top of the inbox to remember it. Start by identifying messages that need an action, are waiting on someone, or are reference material. Use the mock inbox in the downloads; it contains invented roles and `.example` addresses only.

Gmail labels help group related messages. Unlike a traditional folder, one message can have more than one label. A small set is easier to maintain than dozens. For a beginner, consider labels such as `Action`, `Waiting`, `Orders`, `Team` and `Reference`. These are examples, not required answers. Use colour only as a visual cue; do not make colour the only way to distinguish urgent work.

Stars and importance markers can surface a small number of messages, but they are not a task system. If an email needs a deadline, put the action in a task or calendar entry. If it is done, archive it when you no longer need it in the inbox. Archive removes the inbox label; it does not delete the message. Delete only when you are sure you do not need the record.

Gmail search operators narrow results. The official help page currently documents `from:`, `to:`, `subject:`, `has:attachment`, `older_than:` and `is:unread`. Try `from:orders@freshbites.example subject:reservation` against the fictional set. Combine terms carefully; if no results appear, remove one condition at a time. Search is often safer than manually scanning a long inbox.

Filters apply rules to messages that meet a condition. A filter may label, star, archive, forward or delete messages, depending on the options selected. Create a test filter that labels only a known fictional sender. Review the search condition and actions before saving. Avoid filters that automatically delete, forward outside your control or archive security notices. A filter is a rule, not a guarantee that every future message will be classified correctly.

### Lesson 2: Reusable replies and a 15-minute triage

Templates can save time on repeated replies, such as acknowledging an enquiry or asking for missing details. Keep each response short, neutral and editable. A template should not invent availability, a price, a delivery promise or a customer's order. Confirm details before sending. Availability and setup location can change; look for the current Templates setting in Gmail Help rather than relying on a remembered menu path.

Schedule send can help you write at a convenient time and deliver later. Undo Send provides a short cancellation window after sending; check its current setting before depending on it. Neither feature corrects an incorrect recipient. Before you send, check the address, subject, attachment and whether the content contains private information. For sensitive information, use an approved channel rather than an ordinary email attachment.

A practical inbox-zero routine is a short review, not a demand to have zero messages:

1. **Scan** for security notices, deadlines and urgent requests.
2. **Do** an action that takes a minute or less, if safe and clear.
3. **Defer** longer work to a dated task or calendar block.
4. **Delegate** only through an approved process and with the right permissions.
5. **File** useful reference material with a label or archive it.
6. **Delete or report spam** only when you recognise it and do not need the record.

Try a 15-minute timer. At the end, note unresolved items rather than rushing a risky response. Email delegation, where available, gives another account a defined way to manage mail; it is not permission to share a password. It may be disabled or restricted for personal accounts. This course treats delegation as an optional concept only; do not grant it for the assignment.

> **Warning:** An unexpected email asking for a password, verification code, payment or urgent account recovery is not made safe by a familiar logo. Open account security directly in a new tab; do not follow a suspicious link or approve an unexpected sign-in.

### Lesson 3: Calendar as a plan, not a list of promises

Calendar events reserve time and make it easier for invited people to coordinate. Give an event a clear title, start and end time, time zone, location or meeting link, and a short agenda when needed. For a recurring event, review the end date and exceptions; an indefinite weekly event can create clutter or invite people after the routine has changed.

Set the Calendar time zone to the one you actually use. The example team is in Yenagoa, so the practice event uses `Africa/Lagos`. If you work with someone in another region, add or display a second time zone where the current interface allows it. Confirm the date and local time before sending an invitation, especially around daylight-saving changes in the other participant's region.

Use the event details to make a useful invitation: what the meeting is for, what to prepare and who needs to attend. Add a video meeting only if the participants have agreed to meet online. Review guest permissions; participants may be able to invite others or modify details depending on the settings. Do not add a real person's address without permission.

Appointment schedules let people book available time from a booking page. As checked on 12 November 2026, Google's help says a personal Google Account or Workspace account and a computer browser can create a basic schedule, while some features require an eligible Workspace or Google One subscription. Treat appointment schedules as optional and check [current requirements](https://support.google.com/calendar/answer/10729749) in your account. A normal calendar event is sufficient for this course.

Use working hours or a weekly routine to make commitments realistic. Block study, family, travel and rest time before adding optional work. A calendar is visible to the people you share it with; check the calendar's sharing level and event visibility before putting private details in titles or descriptions.

### Lesson 4: Drive, file ownership and sharing

Create a predictable folder structure from the template. Numbered folders help a team scan in order, while a single `Archive` prevents old drafts from mixing with current work. Use dates and version numbers consistently, and give files names that explain what they contain. Avoid names like `final-final-latest`.

In My Drive, files usually belong to the individual account. In **Shared with me**, you see items someone else shared; that view is not necessarily a folder you own. If you no longer need a shared file, remove your access or ask the owner to do so; do not delete a file you do not own. A **Shared drive** is designed for team ownership so files can remain with the organisation when a person leaves. Shared drives require an eligible organisation Workspace setup and are not necessary for this course. Do not create a paid Workspace domain just to try them.

Drive roles include Viewer, Commenter and Editor for many file types. A Viewer can read, a Commenter can add feedback where supported, and an Editor can change content. A folder's access can affect files inside it, so inspect both the folder and any special file-level sharing. Start with **Restricted**. "Anyone with the link" means the link can be forwarded; it does not mean only your intended recipient can open it.

For the assignment, use a second account only if you control it or have explicit permission. Give it Viewer access, open the file from that account to test, then remove access. Never put real customer, student or financial data in a file used for link testing. Version history can help recover or compare changes in Google files, but it does not replace a separate copy. For a critical file, keep a backup outside the same shared location.

### Lesson 5: Contacts, Tasks and Keep

Contacts stores the names and details you choose to keep. Add only information you have a reason and permission to retain. Tasks is useful for a short action with a due date; Keep is useful for quick notes and checklists. They solve different problems:

| Tool | Good fit | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Contacts | A consented work contact you need to reach | Importing every address without a purpose |
| Tasks | A concrete next action with a due date | Vague goals such as "fix everything" |
| Keep | A quick private note or reusable checklist | Passwords, codes or confidential client data |

Try a task called "Review Fresh Bites practice proposal" with a due date. Use a Keep checklist for the steps in the 15-minute triage. If you connect a note or task to Calendar, confirm its visibility and due date. Product labels and app availability can vary across web and mobile.

### Lesson 6: Security habits that fit everyday work

Use a unique password or passkey and enable 2-Step Verification where available. Keep recovery methods current. Review account security directly from the Google Account settings page. Never give a caller, mentor or message sender a password or one-time code. If an unexpected Google prompt appears, deny it and check activity through the official account page.

Phishing messages often pressure you to act quickly, ask you to open an attachment, or send you to a lookalike sign-in page. Hovering over a link can reveal its destination on a computer, but do not open it to "test" it. Check the sender and context, navigate to the service yourself, and ask a known contact through a separate channel. A legitimate-looking sender name is not proof of legitimacy.

## Common mistakes

- Creating many labels and filters before understanding what each one does.
- Automatically archiving or deleting messages that may contain account alerts.
- Treating a calendar invitation as consent to share a private calendar.
- Setting Drive access to "anyone with the link" because it is quicker.
- Assuming offline access is active without testing a file while connected.
- Saving a password, recovery code or real customer detail in Keep or a training document.
- Using an appointment booking page without checking audience, time zone and plan requirements.

1. Sort the 30 fictional messages into a small label system. Write one sentence explaining each label's purpose.
2. Search the mock set for unread, older and attachment examples; record which operator you used.
3. Create three draft templates: acknowledgement, request for missing details and meeting follow-up. Do not send them.
4. Design three filters for fictional mail. Test each search condition before applying an action.
5. Add a weekly study routine and one 30-minute team event in the `Africa/Lagos` time zone.
6. Build the folder structure template and save one copy of each Week 1 starter.
7. Write a three-step response to an unexpected sign-in prompt without sharing a code.

## Facilitator Notes

Ask learners to use their own practice account only. Never ask them to share a password, code, recovery key, inbox screenshot containing real mail or access to a private folder. If a student's organisation account blocks a feature, accept a written permission plan as an alternative. Do not ask the learner to change a managed-account policy. Keep the 15-minute triage exercise focused on safe decision-making rather than inbox-zero speed.
