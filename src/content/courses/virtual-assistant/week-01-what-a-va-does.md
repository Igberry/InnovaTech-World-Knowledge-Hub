**Goal:** Get a clear, honest picture of what virtual assistants actually do, see the range of services
and specialisms, understand what the work really pays, and write a first version of your own
positioning line.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this week you can:

- Explain in plain words what a virtual assistant is and does
- Name at least eight common VA services and group them into general and specialist work
- Describe three common VA niches and who they serve
- Talk honestly about what VA work pays and what affects it
- Write a one-line positioning statement for yourself

## Lesson 1: What a virtual assistant actually is

A **virtual assistant (VA)** is someone who does support tasks for a business or busy person, remotely,
over the internet. "Virtual" just means you are not in their office; "assistant" means you take work off
their plate so they can focus on what only they can do.

Think of a founder who runs a small coaching business. They are great at coaching, but their day fills
up with email, scheduling, chasing invoices, and tidy admin. Every hour they spend on that is an hour
they are not coaching (and not earning). A VA takes that admin away. The founder gets time back; you get
paid for being organised and reliable.

That is the whole deal, and it is a real, honest service. You are not pretending to be anything. You are
the person who makes a busy person's week work.

> **Note:** "Virtual assistant" is a broad label. Two VAs can do completely different work: one manages
> inboxes and calendars, another runs a client's social media. The skill is the same underneath: being
> organised, reliable, and easy to work with.

## Lesson 2: The two kinds of VA work

Most VA work falls into two groups. You will usually start in the first and grow into the second.

**General (administrative) VA work** - the everyday tasks almost every business needs:

- Inbox and email management
- Calendar management and scheduling
- Data entry and clean-up
- Travel booking and research
- Document formatting and file organisation
- Simple customer replies

**Specialist VA work** - a focused skill that pays more because fewer people do it well:

- Social media management (scheduling, replies, simple graphics)
- E-commerce support (orders, listings, customer questions)
- Customer support (help desk, FAQs)
- Light bookkeeping (invoices, receipts, expense tracking)
- Executive assistance (managing a senior person's whole workflow)

| | General VA | Specialist VA |
|---|---|---|
| Who needs it | Almost every business | Businesses in a specific area |
| How hard to start | Easier | Needs a focused skill |
| Typical pay | Lower to start | Higher as you prove it |
| Good for | Your first clients | Your second year |

> **Tip:** Start general to get your first client and real experience. Pick a specialism (Week 5) once
> you know which tasks you enjoy and are good at. Specialists earn more and compete with fewer people.

## Lesson 3: Honest income expectations

You will see big income claims online. Treat them with care. What a VA earns depends on your skills,
your speed, your niche, the clients you find, and how consistently you work and apply.

Here is the honest version:

- When you are new, you earn less because you are still learning how long tasks take and building proof.
- As you get faster, gather reviews, and specialise, you can charge more for the same hours.
- Income is rarely steady at the start. One client can leave; another can double their hours. Plan for
  ups and downs, and aim to work with more than one client over time.

Rather than chase a number, focus on three things you control: **get genuinely good at the work, make
your value easy to see, and apply consistently.** The income follows those, not the other way round.

> **Warning:** Anyone promising you a fixed, high income "guaranteed" as a new VA is selling a dream,
> not the truth. This course will not promise you a salary. It will make you genuinely capable, which is
> what actually gets and keeps clients.

You will build a proper pricing worksheet in Week 6. For now, just know that your first rate is a
starting point you will raise as you prove yourself.

## Lesson 4: Who clients are and what they want

Your future clients are usually small business owners, coaches, consultants, online sellers, agencies,
or busy professionals. They are drowning in small tasks and want to hand them off.

Across all of them, clients want the same few things from a VA:

1. **Reliability** - you do what you said, by when you said.
2. **Clear communication** - they always know what is done and what you need from them.
3. **Care with detail** - you catch the small mistakes before they do.
4. **Trust** - you handle their inbox, money, and private information safely.

Notice what is not on that list: being a genius, or knowing every tool. Those matter less than being
someone a client can relax about. That is good news; reliability and care are things you can choose to
offer from day one.

### Common mistakes

- **Trying to offer everything.** "I do all admin tasks for anyone" is forgettable. Specific is stronger.
- **Undervaluing admin.** Clients pay real money to get their time back. This is valuable work.
- **Believing the income hype.** Build skills and proof first; the money follows.
- **Thinking you need experience to start.** You need skill and reliability. You can build proof without
  a client yet (you will do this in the portfolio week).

## Lesson 5: Write your positioning line

A **positioning line** is one sentence that says who you help and with what. It makes you memorable and
easy to refer. The simple formula:

> "I help [who] with [what] so they can [benefit]."

Examples (write your own, do not copy these):

- "I help busy coaches keep a clean inbox and a calendar they can trust."
- "I help small online stores answer customers quickly and keep orders tidy."
- "I help consultants get their admin off their plate so they can focus on clients."

Open the [service menu worksheet](/downloads/virtual-assistant/service-menu-pricing.md) and draft your
positioning line at the top. It will change as you learn; that is fine. Having one now gives you a focus.

## Practice Exercises

1. List ten tasks a busy small-business owner might want to hand off. Mark each as general or specialist.
2. From that list, circle three tasks you could do well today.
3. Find two real VA job posts online (search "virtual assistant remote" on a job board). Write down the
   exact tasks each one asks for.
4. Write three different positioning lines for yourself, each for a different kind of client.
5. Pick the positioning line that feels most "you" and explain in two sentences why.
6. Describe, in your own words, the difference between a general and a specialist VA.
7. Write down one honest sentence about VA income you could say to a friend who asked "can you get rich
   doing this?"

## Facilitator Notes

Many learners arrive either over-confident (expecting fast riches) or under-confident (thinking they
need years of experience). Spend time on Lesson 3. Celebrate specific positioning lines and gently push
vague ones ("I do all admin for everyone") toward a clear who and what. If a learner cannot name three
tasks they are good at, help them find them; everyone has some. Keep the tone encouraging and honest.
