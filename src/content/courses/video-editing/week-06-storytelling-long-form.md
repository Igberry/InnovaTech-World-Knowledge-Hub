**Goal:** Edit a two-to-three-minute story or event highlight with a clear structure, useful B-roll, understandable sound and organised project files.

## Learning Objectives

- Plan a short story using hook, context, build, payoff and closing.
- Write a two-column script and storyboard that connect sound to picture.
- Record a consented interview or voice-over with useful B-roll and clean audio.
- Organise footage in bins and work through rough, fine and final cuts.
- Use DaVinci Resolve's Cut/Edit, Color, Fairlight and Deliver work areas conceptually where available.
- Apply a low-spec workflow and request feedback that is specific and safe.

## Tools / Accounts Needed This Week

DaVinci Resolve is the main editor for this week. A phone and the Week 1 project folder are required; a computer that can open the installed version is strongly preferred. Shotcut or CapCut is an acceptable alternative if hardware or operating system support prevents use of Resolve. ## Lessons

### Lesson 1: Story structure

A short video story needs a central idea. Write it in one sentence before collecting more clips. A useful structure is **hook, context, build, payoff and closing**. The hook introduces a question or detail. Context tells the viewer what is happening. The build develops the process or problem. The payoff resolves or clarifies it. The closing leaves the viewer with a useful idea or approved next step.

This structure is flexible. A two-minute event highlight may begin with an evocative moment, then show where the event takes place, meaningful moments, and an ending. A brand story may introduce a craft, explain why a process matters, and show a result. Do not invent a history or claim to know a person's motivation. Ask the subject how they would describe it and confirm the wording.

Separate the **story** from the shot list. The story is what the viewer should understand; shots are evidence and images that help tell it. If the subject is “how a small craft is made,” footage of unrelated decorations may look attractive but not advance the story. Keep only the material that makes the central idea clearer.

### Lesson 2: Script and storyboard

A two-column script places the spoken words on one side and the picture or B-roll on the other. It helps avoid recording a paragraph with no matching visuals. A storyboard adds a sketch or frame description for each scene. The drawing can be simple boxes; it is a planning aid, not finished artwork.

Write concise sentences that sound natural when spoken. Mark pauses and pronunciation notes. For an interview, prepare open questions and listen rather than trying to force a memorised answer. Ask permission before recording and clarify where the video may appear. Do not ask for sensitive information or coach someone into a false testimonial.

Plan cutaways: hands preparing materials, a tool being used, a safe exterior establishing shot, or a close detail. B-roll can cover a cut or illustrate a spoken point. It must not be used to imply a different event or location. If footage is from another date, label it in notes and do not present it as live coverage.

### Lesson 3: Interview shooting basics

Frame a consenting interviewee at a comfortable distance. Place the camera near eye level and leave some space in the direction the person is looking. A background should provide context without exposing private information or distracting detail. Natural window light can work; place the subject so the face is not lost against a bright window.

Record a sound test and listen with headphones. Put the phone or microphone close enough for a clear voice, keep it stable and avoid handling noise. Ask one question at a time, wait for the full answer, and let the person finish. If an answer is unclear, ask a neutral follow-up rather than supplying words for them.

Get the participant's permission for the specific use. An interviewee may agree to a classroom recording but not public posting. Explain whether it is a practice edit, portfolio piece or client delivery. Record the permission status in the project notes. For this course, avoid minors; any legitimate work involving children requires guardian consent and compliance with venue rules.

### Lesson 4: B-roll and cutaways

B-roll should illustrate an idea or help an edit flow. If a person says, “I check each detail,” show a detail being checked only if that is what the footage depicts. Do not use archival footage as though it happened during the interview. Keep a clip log with date, location, permission and what the shot shows.

Capture more than one distance where possible: an establishing view, medium action, close-up and detail. Record stable clips long enough to make editing possible. Avoid needless camera movement. Check reflections, signs and screens. If the subject is a real business, make sure the owner approves any product label, address or employee who appears.

### Lesson 5: Long-edit stages and Resolve orientation

A long edit is easier in stages. A **string-out** gathers candidate clips in a rough order. A **rough cut** establishes story and timing. A **fine cut** adjusts pacing, continuity, sound and graphics. The **final review** checks access, permissions, captions, export and delivery.

Resolve organizes related work into different pages or work areas. The Media area helps import and organize files in bins. Cut and Edit work areas are used to assemble and refine a timeline. Color provides colour controls; Fairlight focuses on audio; Deliver prepares exports. Names and layout can change by version. Learn the goal of each work area and verify the current user guide rather than memorizing steps from a different release.

Create bins or folders for interviews, B-roll, audio, graphics and sequences. Give clips useful names or metadata. Keep source media in the project folders and ensure the software can reconnect it. Before a large import, make a small test project and check that playback and saving work. Do not move the raw files after linking them without relinking the project.

### Lesson 6: Fine editing, colour and audio

Build the story before adding polish. Remove repeated information, unrelated shots and long gaps. Preserve the person's meaning and context. If a statement is shortened, play the result with surrounding sentences and confirm it is not misleading.

Use colour correction to make shots consistent, not to disguise an event or appearance. Check exposure, white balance and saturation against a neutral reference where possible. If a clip is very dark or badly mixed in lighting, a correction may not recover missing detail. Note the limitation instead of pushing the image until it looks unnatural.

In the audio work area, check dialogue first, then natural sound and music. Avoid clipping and abrupt level changes. Use only music with permission for the intended use and log the terms. Add captions or subtitles and proofread them. A voice-over can help when an interview is not available, but it must describe actual footage accurately.

### Lesson 7: Low-spec workflow

Longer projects can strain a laptop. Check available storage before importing. Keep source files on a local drive with room for project files, cache and exports. Close unneeded apps and use a shorter timeline while learning.

If the editor supports **proxy** or **optimised media**, it can create smaller working copies for smoother playback while retaining the original for final output. Confirm the proxy folder, file naming and reconnect behavior. Do not delete the originals after proxy generation. If the feature is missing, lower preview resolution if available, edit fewer clips at once, or switch to a lighter editor such as Shotcut. The exact menu labels and performance depend on the current build and hardware.

Another option is to assemble the story using a phone workflow, provided the deliverable remains accessible and the project can be backed up. For this week's assignment, Resolve is preferred but not a barrier to completion. Record the device constraint, the alternative used and what you would need to revisit on a stronger computer.

### Lesson 8: Feedback and editing log

Ask a reviewer a focused question: “Is the opening clear?” or “Does the voice remain understandable?” Avoid asking only “Do you like it?” A reviewer can identify confusion, but the editor makes the final decision against the brief. Record the feedback and the change you accept or decline.

The editing log creates an honest record of choices. It can describe removing a repeated answer, moving a B-roll shot to support a line, or shortening an ending. It should not be used to rationalize an inaccurate edit. Keep a version before and after significant changes so you can restore a choice.

## Common Mistakes

- Starting with a montage before deciding the story's central idea.
- Asking leading interview questions or editing a response out of context.
- Using B-roll from another event as if it were recorded during the interview.
- Overloading bins with duplicate files and losing track of originals.
- Adding colour effects and music before the story and dialogue are clear.
- Deleting source footage after generating proxies.
- Assuming a laptop is inadequate without testing a short sequence and preview settings.

## Practice Exercises

1. Write a one-sentence story idea and choose a hook, context, build and payoff.
2. Turn the story into a two-column script and six-frame storyboard.
3. Record a 20-second practice answer with permission, then capture two supporting B-roll shots.
4. Sort sample clips into bins and make a short string-out.
5. Make a rough cut and remove one repeated idea without changing meaning.
6. Compare original and corrected shots for exposure and white balance.
7. Test a proxy or lower-preview workflow on a short copy if available.
8. Ask a reviewer one focused question and record the change in the editing log.

## Weekly Assignment (Due Before Week 7)

Create a two-to-three-minute brand story or small event highlight, such as the fictional story of Yenagoa Fresh Bites or an event for which you have permission. Use Resolve if the computer supports it; otherwise use Shotcut or CapCut and note why. Include an interview or voice-over, relevant B-roll, licensed or omitted music, titles and captions. Submit the video, a script and a short editing log describing what you cut and why.

**Grading guide:** story structure 30%, editing quality 25%, audio and colour 20%, script and log 15%, technical delivery 10%.

**Definition of done**

- [ ] The video is two to three minutes with a clear central story.
- [ ] Interview or voice-over is permission-cleared and intelligible.
- [ ] B-roll supports the story without misrepresenting when or where it was captured.
- [ ] Captions, titles, colour and sound have been reviewed.
- [ ] Music has a suitable licence or is omitted and explained.
- [ ] Script and editing log are submitted; source and project files remain organised.
