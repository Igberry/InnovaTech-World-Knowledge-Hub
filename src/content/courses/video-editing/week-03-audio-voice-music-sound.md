**Goal:** Re-edit the Week 2 video with intelligible voice-over, carefully balanced audio and documented permission for every added sound.

## Learning Objectives

- Explain why clear audio is essential to an effective video.
- Write and record a concise 20–30 second voice-over in a quiet environment.
- Use meters and listening checks to avoid clipping and inaudible dialogue.
- Balance dialogue, music and natural sound, using ducking or manual volume changes.
- Describe the purpose of noise reduction, equalisation and normalisation without over-processing.
- Keep a licence log and use only sounds permitted for the specific intended use.

## Tools / Accounts Needed This Week

Use the Week 2 project, a phone or microphone, headphones and the voice-over template. CapCut remains the primary editor, but the exact audio tools depend on version and device. An offline, learner-recorded voice is sufficient; a social account or paid music library is not required. Use only a sound you recorded yourself or one whose terms clearly allow this use. The official YouTube Audio Library guidance is listed in Resources, but read the current track-specific terms before using any item.

## Lessons

### Lesson 1: Why audio can make or break a video

Audio carries speech, location and mood. A viewer may accept a simple frame if the story is clear, but noisy or distorted speech can make the message impossible to follow. Before applying effects, listen for the cause: distance, background noise, wind, handling, clipping, room echo or a mismatch in levels. Editing cannot reliably reconstruct words that were never recorded clearly.

Start each project with the audio plan. Decide whether the video needs dialogue, natural process sound, room tone, music or effects. For a short food or craft clip, natural sound may be enough. For a narrated explanation, the voice should be understandable before adding music. Do not add sound merely to make the timeline look busy.

### Lesson 2: Write and record a concise voice-over

A 20–30 second voice-over usually contains one main idea. Write for the ear rather than the page: short sentences, familiar words and natural pauses. Read aloud once, remove repeated phrases and practise unfamiliar names. The script template provides a space for each beat and its matching image.

Choose a quiet location with soft furnishings where possible. Hard, empty rooms produce reflections and echo; curtains, clothing or cushions can absorb some reflections. These materials do not eliminate traffic or machinery, so record a test. Keep the phone or microphone near enough for a clear voice but outside the camera frame. Point the microphone as intended by its design, avoid touching the device and maintain a steady distance.

Record several short takes rather than one long take. Leave a pause before and after each sentence so the editor has room to trim. If you make a mistake, pause and repeat the sentence; do not stop the recording immediately, because the quiet gap helps you locate the retake. Record room tone for a few seconds. Label takes with project, line and version, and save the best source in `02-Audio`.

Never record another person's voice secretly. Do not include an interview or testimonial unless the person understands the intended use and agrees. A fictional Fresh Bites script must not be presented as a real customer quote or factual business claim.

### Lesson 3: Levels, clipping and listening

An audio meter shows signal level. If it reaches the top and sounds harsh, the recording may be **clipping**: the signal exceeded the available range and has distorted. Lowering a clipped file after recording does not restore the missing sound shape. Record another take at a safer level or move the microphone farther away if the source is loud.

If a signal is extremely quiet, increasing it later can also make room noise more noticeable. Set the input level where available, make a short test and listen. No single number works for every device and app; use the meter as a guide, avoid the top, and check intelligibility on a real export. Dialogue should be audible at a comfortable listening level without forcing the viewer to turn the volume all the way up.

Use headphones to identify clicks, hum, wind or mouth noise. Then check the mix on a phone speaker or another playback device if available. Headphones reveal detail, while a small speaker can expose dialogue that disappears in the full mix. Do not rely only on waveform size or meters; the human ear and a second playback check matter.

### Lesson 4: Editing and syncing sound

Place the voice-over near the picture it explains. If recording separately, align it with the timeline using a clear start point, a visual cue or an intentional action. Listen across each edit. A sudden change in background tone can reveal a cut even when the image is smooth. Room tone or a natural sound bed may help, but do not loop it so obviously that it distracts.

Trim breaths only when they are distracting; speech needs natural pauses. Keep the sense and timing of a sentence intact. Avoid rearranging words so the speaker appears to claim something they did not say. For an interview, preserve context and do not cut an answer to reverse its meaning.

### Lesson 5: Music, mood and cutting to a beat

Music can support mood and pacing, but it can also compete with the message. Choose music only after the picture and voice work. A slower or simpler sound may suit a careful demonstration; a more energetic track may suit a quick process. Avoid using music to imply an unsupported emotional or factual claim.

Music rights are specific. A track in an app, a search result, a social platform library or a “royalty-free” collection is not automatically licensed for every account, country, business, client, monetised video or cross-platform export. Read the current licence for the exact track and intended use. Keep the source, creator, terms, date checked and required attribution in the licence log. The official YouTube Audio Library help page is a starting point, not a blanket grant for every use. If the terms are unclear, do not use the track.

Cutting to the beat means placing selected edits near a musical pulse. It can create cohesion, but it is not a rule. Do not speed up every cut just to match music, and never let a beat dictate an unsafe or misleading edit. For this assignment, music is optional if no suitable licensed track is available.

### Lesson 6: Ducking and sound effects

**Ducking** lowers music or another background sound while dialogue is present. Some editors automate this; in others, you can create volume keyframes or separate the music into sections. The exact feature varies. Listen to the result: the music should not swell and drop so abruptly that the viewer notices the control rather than the story.

Manual adjustment can be enough. Lower the music under speech, raise it slightly in a pause only if it remains comfortable, and use short fades at the beginning and end. Leave natural sound where it adds context. A sound effect should correspond to something that happens or clearly function as a creative accent. Do not add a dramatic impact, applause or crowd reaction that falsely suggests an event took place.

### Lesson 7: Noise reduction, EQ and normalisation

**Noise reduction** attempts to reduce steady background sound. It can create watery or metallic artifacts when set too strongly. **Equalisation (EQ)** changes frequency balance; a small adjustment may clarify voice, but aggressive changes can make it thin or harsh. **Normalisation** adjusts a clip or mix toward a selected target level; it does not fix bad microphone placement, clipping or noise.

Feature names and controls differ, and some may be restricted by a paid plan. Make a duplicate before processing. Apply the smallest change that helps, compare processed and unprocessed audio at similar playback loudness, and undo if quality gets worse. Use the tools as a final polish, not a replacement for recording in a good location.

### Lesson 8: Licence log, sync and final review

For each sound asset, record a useful identifier and source. If the sound was recorded by you, note who recorded it and whether other voices or property are involved. For licensed music, capture the licence or permission, exact use allowed, any attribution, checked date and evidence saved. The licence may prohibit commercial use, editing, redistribution or use on some platforms. Do not infer permissions from an icon or a creator's description alone.

Sync separately recorded audio by aligning a visible action, clap or spoken cue, if it is appropriate and consented. Remove a temporary cue only after sync is correct. If the audio drifts over a long recording, check whether sample rates, frame rates or device clocks differ; recapture short sections if a beginner workflow cannot reliably fix it.

Before delivery, listen to the full video with headphones and then a second playback option. Check that the voice is clear, music is not masking speech, no sound is clipped, edits are smooth, and every asset is logged. Export a new review copy and inspect it; do not assume the timeline preview equals the final file.

## Common Mistakes

- Recording voice-over in a noisy room and expecting one filter to remove everything.
- Holding a phone too far away or moving it during a take.
- Raising a clipped recording instead of capturing a clean replacement.
- Setting music by waveform appearance without listening to the mix.
- Assuming an in-app track is cleared for every commercial or platform use.
- Applying strong noise reduction or EQ without comparing the original.
- Editing spoken words to change the speaker's meaning.
- Forgetting to document self-recorded sounds, permissions and attribution.

## Practice Exercises

1. Record the same sentence from two distances and compare clarity and room noise.
2. Write a 20–30 second script and remove words that do not support its one idea.
3. Record three takes and choose the clearest one; note why.
4. Add voice-over to a copy of the Week 2 edit and align each sentence with a useful shot.
5. Compare a mix with and without music; describe what improves or becomes harder to hear.
6. Try a gentle fade or ducking control if available; otherwise make manual volume changes.
7. Compare original and lightly processed audio at similar loudness.
8. Fill one sample row in the licence log and identify which terms still need checking.

## Weekly Assignment (Due Before Week 4)

Record a 20–30 second voice-over script and re-edit the Week 2 video with the voice-over, music and one sound effect. Use music only if its current licence permits the intended use; otherwise document that you omitted music and why. Apply ducking or a manual volume change so speech remains clear. Submit the new edit, the script and a licence log that records each added sound's source, licence terms, date and attribution or permission details.

**Grading guide:** audio clarity 30%, levels and ducking 25%, music choice and licence log 25%, script quality 20%.

**Definition of done**

- [ ] The voice-over is 20–30 seconds and understandable in the full export.
- [ ] Music and sound effect are licensed for the intended use or explicitly omitted.
- [ ] The mix is reviewed on headphones and a second playback option if available.
- [ ] The licence log is complete, including self-recorded and omitted assets where relevant.
- [ ] The edit remains a coherent 30-second piece with no unsupported claims.
