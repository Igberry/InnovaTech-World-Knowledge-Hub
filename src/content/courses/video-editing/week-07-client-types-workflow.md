**Goal:** Manage a client-style video project from discovery through two documented revision rounds and an organised hand-off.

## Learning Objectives

- Identify common client video types and the information each brief should contain.
- Ask discovery questions about audience, purpose, approval, rights and delivery.
- Plan a safe shoot with location permission, participant consent and an event checklist.
- Produce versions 1, 2 and 3 using two rounds of supplied feedback.
- Use time-coded notes, version naming and a change log to control revisions.
- Prepare an accurate title, thumbnail concept, description and delivery report without inventing claims.

## Tools / Accounts Needed This Week

Use the fictional choir or Fresh Bites brief, the client brief template, Week 6 editing files and an editor already installed. No real client, venue, church, school or participant is required. If a real subject is used, obtain permission for the specific use and follow the organiser's rules. A discovery call can be role-played. Do not send an actual quote or agreement as part of this exercise.

## Lessons

### Lesson 1: Common client video types

Different goals call for different footage and review criteria. An **event highlight** may show the approved setting, selected activities and atmosphere. A **product promotion** should show the product accurately and avoid unsupported claims. A **testimonial** requires a consenting speaker and an honest edit. A **tutorial or explainer** needs a clear sequence, close details and captions. An **organisation profile** may combine an interview, work process and context.

Other examples include a school event, church programme, wedding, graduation, NGO awareness video, creator introduction or recruitment explainer. These are examples of formats, not promises of clients or income. A video for a sensitive subject needs additional planning and may not be suitable for beginner practice. Ask who is allowed to be filmed and what must remain private.

### Lesson 2: Discovery, brief and approval

A **discovery conversation** identifies the reason for the project before discussing shots. Ask who the video is for, what the viewer should know, where it will be used, what facts are approved, what the deadline is, who reviews it and how success will be judged. Clarify what is out of scope. The supplied discovery questions help keep the conversation focused.

Turn the answers into a written brief. Include audience, key message, deliverables, length, destination, approval process, filming location, participants, captions, assets, licence responsibilities, review rounds and hand-off. If any fact is missing, mark it as a question rather than inventing it. Ask the client to approve the brief before filming.

Do not collect passwords, payment card information or unnecessary identity details. Use a written contact method that the client approves. If the project involves private information, ask what storage and retention rules apply. This course does not provide legal advice; the simple agreement outline is a learning aid, not a contract.

### Lesson 3: Consent and sensitive settings

People in a video deserve to understand what is being recorded and how it may be used. Obtain permission before close-ups or interviews. A person attending an event has not automatically agreed to appear in a public promotional video. Respect anyone who says no; use another angle or omit the footage.

For children, obtain guardian permission and follow venue rules. This course's safest default is not to film children. Religious and cultural settings may have restrictions on filming, music, dress or publication. Ask the organiser rather than assuming. Do not film private prayer, medical activity, grief or a sensitive conversation as B-roll without explicit, informed permission and a clear reason.

Public space is not permission to film everything. Avoid blocking paths, entrances, exits and activities. Never expose a private address, phone number, identity badge, payment record or screen. If a drone or gimbal is considered, pause and check current local rules, venue approval, safety requirements and weather. Drone operation is not taught here; no local regulation is asserted.

### Lesson 4: The client workflow

A safe workflow reduces confusion:

1. **Discovery:** clarify purpose, audience, destination and constraints.
2. **Brief and quote:** document deliverables, schedule, revision count and responsibilities.
3. **Approval and deposit:** agree the terms in writing before committing significant time.
4. **Pre-production:** create a script, shot list, permissions plan, schedule and backup plan.
5. **Shoot:** follow venue rules and log footage, releases and assets.
6. **Edit version 1:** make a review copy and ask for one authorised decision-maker.
7. **Revisions:** collect consolidated, time-coded notes; create version 2 and version 3.
8. **Approval and delivery:** confirm final version, usage terms, hand-off and archive instructions.

The exercise uses three versions because there are two revision rounds: V1 is the initial cut, V2 applies Round 1, and V3 applies Round 2. A new concept or new footage request may exceed the original scope. Record it and ask for a revised brief rather than silently doing extra work.

### Lesson 5: Review rounds and change logs

Ask reviewers to refer to the displayed timecode and describe the desired result. “At 00:14, shorten the pause after the title” is more actionable than “make it pop.” Collect one consolidated list from the agreed reviewer; conflicting feedback from many people can produce an inconsistent edit.

For each note, mark **applied**, **adapted**, **declined** or **needs clarification**. Explain the decision in the change log. A revision request can be declined if it would make the story inaccurate, violate a licence, expose private information or exceed the agreed scope. Explain calmly and propose a safe alternative.

Use clear names such as `ChoirPromo_v01_review.mp4`, `v02_review.mp4` and `v03_final.mp4`. The fictional brief must never be confused with a real organisation. Keep project files, review notes and exports together. Do not overwrite the previous version before the new one has been checked.

### Lesson 6: Reusable templates and speed

Reusable title layouts, checklists and folder structures can reduce repetitive setup. A template should contain only elements you are authorised to reuse. Do not include an old client's logo, footage, music or private information in a new project template. Keep templates generic and label placeholders clearly.

Shortcuts can make editing faster, but only after you know what an action does. Use the current app's menus and help to confirm a shortcut. A shortcut may differ across keyboard layout, operating system and version. If you cannot remember it, use the menu rather than risking a destructive edit.

### Lesson 7: YouTube basics and publishing review

A YouTube title should accurately describe the video and help a viewer decide whether it is relevant. A thumbnail should be readable at small size, use permitted images and avoid a misleading reaction or result. A description can state the subject, identify approved sources and include a clear next step. Chapters are optional labels that can help navigate a longer video if the current workflow supports them.

Before publication, check current platform requirements for dimensions, duration, captions, music, thumbnail and disclosure. Policies and features change. Review visibility: public, unlisted and private settings have different effects, and a link can be forwarded. Do not upload a client project to a personal account or a public platform without written approval.

### Lesson 8: Event-day readiness and delivery

Before an event, confirm filming areas, prohibited areas, approved participants, schedule, power and backup. Pack only needed equipment and secure it. Check batteries and storage, carry a safe charging plan, and know where raw footage will be copied. Keep a shot list but remain aware of the event; do not obstruct it to get a shot.

After filming, secure the original media, create a second copy, and note any permission limits. Prepare the version requested by the client and a delivery note that lists files, version, destination and any known limitation. Ask the client how long raw files should be retained. Do not keep footage indefinitely if the agreement says to delete it.

## Common Mistakes

- Editing before the client confirms the audience, key message and destination.
- Assuming venue attendance means permission to publish every person's image.
- Taking conflicting feedback from several people without one approver.
- Treating a new concept as an included revision without checking scope.
- Overwriting V1 before V2 or V3 has been verified.
- Using a client logo, interview or event footage in a new portfolio without approval.
- Uploading a draft to a public or personal platform for convenience.
- Making a title or thumbnail more dramatic than the actual video.

## Practice Exercises

1. Role-play a discovery call and complete the fictional brief.
2. Highlight every unknown fact and write a question rather than filling it in.
3. Build a safe event-day checklist and identify one no-film area.
4. Create a version 1 from permitted footage and add a timecode slate for review.
5. Apply the first revision notes and record each decision in the change log.
6. Apply Round 2 and compare v1, v2 and v3 for unintended changes.
7. Draft an accurate title and thumbnail concept for a fictional video.
8. Create a delivery note and retention reminder.

## Weekly Assignment (Due Before Week 8)

Use the fictional choir promo or Fresh Bites brief. Plan, shoot or select permitted footage, edit version 1, then apply both supplied revision rounds to produce v2 and v3. Submit all three exports or review files, a change log, and a one-page project report. If you cannot shoot safely, use your own permitted footage or complete the planning and edit with a facilitator-approved local set.

**Grading guide:** meeting the brief 25%, editing quality 25%, revision handling and change log 25%, professionalism of documents 15%, file naming and delivery 10%.

**Definition of done**

- [ ] The brief is complete and all unknowns are marked for clarification.
- [ ] Version 1, version 2 and version 3 are distinct and labelled.
- [ ] Both revision rounds have a documented response in the change log.
- [ ] Permission, licence and privacy checks are complete.
- [ ] The project report and delivery note identify limitations honestly.
- [ ] No version is published without approval.

## Self-Check Questions

2. Why should revision notes be time-coded and consolidated?
3. What is the difference between a revision and a new scope request?
4. Why is attendance at an event not automatic permission to publish a person's image?
5. What should a delivery note state?
