**Goal:** Capture a varied, stable and well-organised set of phone clips for a short edit.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this week, you can:

- Explain frame rate, resolution, aspect ratio, bitrate and codec in plain language.
- Choose a consistent phone-camera setup that fits a simple editing goal and available storage.
- Capture wide, medium, close-up and detail footage with deliberate light and clear natural sound.
- Plan a ten-clip shot set and record three clips in both horizontal and vertical orientation.
- Obtain appropriate permission and avoid unsafe or private footage.
- Organise original clips in a named project folder and verify a second copy.

## Tools / Accounts Needed This Week

A phone with a camera, local storage and enough battery for a short practice shoot; headphones for playback; the folder template in the setup guide; and a safe subject you own or have permission to film. A laptop is useful but not required this week. You do not need a social media account, cloud upload, paid app or new camera. Use Fresh Bites as a fictional case, or choose a safe, consented object such as a craft or your own product. Do not film a stranger, a child, private property or a business without permission.

## Lessons

### Lesson 1: How a video file represents motion

Video is a sequence of still images shown quickly. A **frame rate** is the number of frames displayed in one second. A higher number can represent smoother motion, but it can also create larger files and may not suit every story. Common values include 24, 25, 30 and 60 frames per second. These are familiar production choices, not a requirement to use them all. For a beginner exercise, choose a frame rate your phone supports and keep it consistent across related clips. Do not convert a project to a higher number expecting missing motion detail to appear.

**Resolution** describes the dimensions of each frame. A 720p image contains fewer pixels than a 1080p image; 4K contains substantially more. More pixels can preserve detail when the footage is viewed or cropped, but usually require more storage and processing. The final image is also affected by focus, light, compression and the display. A well-lit, steady 1080p clip can be more useful than a dark, shaky clip at a larger resolution.

An **aspect ratio** compares frame width with frame height. A 16:9 frame is wider than it is tall and is common for landscape viewing. A 9:16 frame is vertical and suits phone-first viewing. A 1:1 square frame and a 4:5 portrait frame are other common shapes. Platform layouts and safe areas change, so do not treat any ratio as a permanent requirement. Ask where a video will be viewed and check the destination's current specifications before delivery.

**Bitrate** is the amount of data used to represent a second of video. A higher bitrate can preserve more detail at the cost of a larger file; the result also depends on the scene and codec. A **codec** encodes and decodes the video. H.264 and H.265 are examples; their support and efficiency vary across devices and apps. A file container such as MP4 or MOV holds video, audio and metadata; the extension alone does not tell you every property inside.

Compression reduces file size by representing information more compactly. Lossy compression discards some information, so repeatedly exporting and re-compressing a file can lower quality. Keep original camera files and export from the editor project when changes are needed. Do not promise that "4K" means professional quality or that the largest file is automatically best.

### Lesson 2: The editing workflow

A reliable workflow has clear stages:

1. **Plan:** identify the audience, one main idea, permission needs and delivery destination.
2. **Prepare:** charge devices, clear storage safely, test light and sound, and make the folder structure.
3. **Shoot:** capture the planned coverage and a few seconds of handles before and after the action.
4. **Import:** copy originals to `01-Raw`; check that the copy opens before moving or deleting anything.
5. **Assemble:** select the strongest moments and arrange them in a rough order.
6. **Refine:** remove unnecessary pauses, improve pace and use B-roll to clarify or cover a cut.
7. **Audio and graphics:** balance speech and sound, add titles or captions, then check legibility.
8. **Colour and export:** make restrained corrections, export a review copy and inspect the result.
9. **Deliver and archive:** use the agreed name and method, keep project files, and follow retention instructions.

Do not begin with effects. A short sequence with a clear message, stable framing and intelligible sound is a better foundation than a confusing edit with many transitions. Save versions rather than overwriting the only project file. For example, keep a rough cut and a reviewed cut in `04-Projects`, with a brief note describing each.

### Lesson 3: Phone setup and stable footage

Clean the lens with a soft cloth and hold the phone with both hands. Keep elbows close to the body, brace against a stable surface, or use a safe homemade support. A tripod is optional. Do not walk backward while watching the screen. If a movement is necessary, rehearse it slowly and ensure the floor is clear.

Turn on the camera grid if the phone offers one. Use the grid to keep a horizon level and leave breathing room around the subject. Tap the subject to focus and, if the device provides a focus/exposure lock, use it when the subject and lighting remain stable. Camera menus differ by model and operating system; learn the control on your own device rather than following an outdated menu screenshot.

Choose horizontal or vertical before recording. Keep the phone orientation fixed during each take. For the assignment, capture three chosen scenes in both orientations as separate takes. A crop can help adapt a frame, but it cannot recreate content that was never captured outside the original crop. Keep the important subject away from the very edge so both versions remain usable.

Record a brief sample using the chosen quality. Play it back and check focus, exposure, sound and file size. Use the storage estimate in the setup guide to plan; leave a margin because export files, editor projects and temporary files also use storage. Do not delete photographs or personal files just to make space unless they are backed up and you are sure they can be removed.

### Lesson 4: Light that explains the subject

Light shows shape, texture and colour. A window can be a large soft light source. Place the subject facing or angled toward it, then watch for strong shadows and reflections. If a window is behind the subject, the camera may render the subject too dark. Move the subject or camera instead of relying on a filter to fix everything later.

Avoid mixing noticeably different light colours when consistency matters. A warm room bulb and cool daylight may make one side of a product look orange and another blue. Turn off the unnecessary source or move to one consistent area. Do not place a phone or improvised reflector near a flame, hot pan, electrical cable or wet surface. A white card can bounce some existing light back onto a subject; it does not create light.

For a product, move the object slowly and observe how its appearance changes. Keep labels and claims truthful. For a person, ask permission and avoid filming in a way that exposes private surroundings. Check the background and reflections; a mirror or glossy surface may show an address, phone screen or person outside the frame.

### Lesson 5: Audio first

Viewers can tolerate a simple picture more easily than speech they cannot understand. Move the phone closer to the speaker, reduce nearby noise and use a quiet room. A built-in microphone records the room as well as the voice. A short test with headphones can reveal traffic, a fan, clipping or a distant voice before a full recording.

Record **room tone**, a few seconds of the location's ordinary sound without speech. It can help smooth edits. Do not record a private conversation or include voices in a public place without permission. A low-cost earbud microphone may work, but test it; the phone may select another microphone or add handling noise.

Keep the device steady while recording. Avoid touching the phone or cable during a take. If the intended clip has no dialogue, record the natural process sound separately and label it. Never add a sound effect that falsely suggests an event occurred. For this week, usable natural sound is enough; music is not required.

### Lesson 6: Shot sizes, movement and B-roll

A **wide shot** shows the location or overall arrangement. A **medium shot** shows an activity and some context. A **close-up** focuses on a person, object or action. A **detail shot** isolates a small feature such as a texture or tool. An **over-the-shoulder** view looks past a person toward an activity, but check that screens and documents do not reveal information. A **point-of-view** shot shows what the camera operator sees.

**B-roll** is supporting footage used to add context, cover a cut or show details while a voice explains something. If the main shot is a person describing a craft, B-roll might show the person's permitted tools or hands completing a step. B-roll should support the story, not misrepresent what happened. Label footage with date and subject so it is not mistaken for another event.

Movement can be static, a gentle pan, tilt, or a follow. Static shots are easiest to make stable. A pan turns the camera horizontally; a tilt moves it vertically. Move slowly, start and stop with stillness, and do not move simply because the camera can. A gimbal is not needed for this course. If one is used, check the route, battery and surroundings before moving.

### Lesson 7: The ten-clip shot list and "five seconds" rule

The supplied list gives you a beginning, middle and end: an establishing view, process, details, sound and closing image. "Five seconds rule" is a practical minimum for many simple takes, not a law. Some actions need longer; a very short detail may need less. Start recording before the action and hold after it so an editor has room to cut. Do not stop at the exact moment the action begins.

Before recording, say what each shot contributes. If two clips show exactly the same thing, vary the angle, distance or action. Make sure a viewer could understand the sequence without guessing. Record a short natural sound sample if appropriate. Use the checkboxes on the shot list to track what is captured, then watch a few clips before leaving the location. If focus or audio failed, repeat the shot while the setup is still available.

### Lesson 8: Permission, safety, file names and importing

Ask before filming a person or private location. Explain whether the material is for a class, portfolio or public post. A nod to the camera is not a substitute for clear agreement. If someone declines or withdraws before publication, do not use their image or voice. Avoid children in public practice footage; where a legitimate project involves a child, guardian permission and venue rules are required.

Use the permission one-pager as a reminder, not a legal release. Do not show personal screens, identity documents, home addresses, vehicle plates or private conversations. Do not trespass or block walkways. Keep gear secured; avoid unsafe filming near roads, heat, water, cables or crowds.

Make a project folder with `01-Raw`, `02-Audio`, `03-Graphics`, `04-Projects`, `05-Exports` and `06-Archive`. Name files with date, project, content and version. Keep camera originals unchanged and copy them into `01-Raw`. Make a second copy on separate storage where possible and reopen one file from that copy. Import into the editor only after checking that the files are readable. The full folder and backup procedure is in the setup guide.

## Common Mistakes

- Changing frame rate or orientation repeatedly without a reason.
- Treating resolution as a substitute for focus, light or stable framing.
- Starting or stopping a take exactly on the action and leaving no handles.
- Recording speech from too far away and hoping noise reduction will fix it.
- Assuming a platform's current ratio or duration limit from memory.
- Filming people or private places without clear permission.
- Deleting the only original after importing into an editor.
- Shooting a long clip without checking storage, battery or a test sample.

## Practice Exercises

1. Explain frame rate, resolution, aspect ratio and bitrate to a partner using your own words.
2. Record a ten-second test and estimate the storage for a planned shoot.
3. Capture the same object as wide, medium, close-up and detail shots.
4. Record three scenes in both horizontal and vertical orientations.
5. Move a subject relative to a window and compare the light and background.
6. Record a voice or natural sound test in two locations and identify the clearer one.
7. Complete the ten-clip shot list and mark missing coverage before leaving the location.
8. Organise the clips in the folder template, create a second copy and test it.

Shoot the ten-clip set from the supplied shot list using Fresh Bites or your own safe, consented subject. Record both vertical and horizontal versions of three selected clips. Name and organise the files in the project folder template, keep originals, and make a second copy. - [ ] Ten distinct planned clips are present and reviewable.

## Facilitator Notes

Use a safe, non-identifying classroom object if a learner has no permitted subject. Do not require anyone to film their home or upload footage. Demonstrate camera controls on the available devices without implying that every phone has identical features. Keep the mentor's sample footage local and permission-cleared. Allow a storyboard/contact-sheet alternative if a learner cannot safely record.

## Self-Check Questions

1. How are frame rate, resolution and aspect ratio different?
2. Why should a clip begin before the action and continue briefly after it?
3. What can you do to make phone audio clearer before editing?
4. Why is synchronised cloud storage not always a second backup?
5. What should you do if a person does not agree to be filmed?
