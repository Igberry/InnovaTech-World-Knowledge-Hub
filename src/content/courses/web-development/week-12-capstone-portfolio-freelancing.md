**Goal:** Bring it all together, build a client-style **capstone**, polish a **portfolio** that proves your
job-ready skills, and prepare honestly for **freelance and remote work**. You've built four projects; this
week you ship a fifth and package everything professionally. Mostly building and doing.

## What you'll be able to do

- Scope and build a client-style website as a capstone
- Present a polished portfolio: personal site, GitHub profile, pinned projects
- Handle the basics of client work: discovery, scoping, quoting, deposits, handover
- Find work safely and avoid common scams
- Prepare for interviews and a live coding warm-up

## Before you start (about 10 minutes)

- Line up your four earlier projects, you'll link them all from your portfolio this week.
- Warm-up (3 minutes): open your GitHub profile as a stranger would. First impression? That gap is what
  you'll close this week.

## Try it 1 - Scope and build your capstone

**Why it matters:** one substantial, real-feeling project shows employers and clients what you can do.

**Do this now:**
1. Pick one:
   - **Option A, a real client** (a local business, school, church, clinic, NGO) **with written permission**
     to build and showcase their site. Only proceed with permission in writing.
   - **Option B, a realistic case study:** invent a plausible client and write a brief (who they are, what
     they need, their content). Make it believable and complete.
2. Build it: multiple pages, responsive, accessible, deployed, with real content, and where it fits a
   database feature (a contact or booking form saved with Supabase).
3. Write a README and a short case study (the problem, your solution, screenshots).

**You've got it when:** your capstone is deployed, works end to end, and has a written brief or permission
behind it.

## Try it 2 - Practise the client workflow

**Why it matters:** even one paying client needs a process. Getting paid and avoiding disputes depends on it.

**Do this now, draft each of these for your capstone client (real or invented):**
1. **Discovery questions:** who is this for, what must it do, what content exists, when is it needed, what's
   the budget?
2. **A written scope:** exactly what's included (and what's not).
3. **Pricing:** pick a fixed price per project (best for beginners) or an hourly rate; research local and
   remote rates and price for your level honestly.
4. **Terms:** a **50% deposit before you start**, the rest on delivery; an agreed number of revision rounds;
   a handover plan.

**You've got it when:** you have the scope, price, deposit and timeline written down.

> **Important:** put the scope, price, deposit and timeline **in writing** (even a simple message) before
> starting. "In writing" prevents most disputes.

## Try it 3 - Polish your portfolio and GitHub

**Why it matters:** recruiters and clients judge you on what they can see in the first 30 seconds.

**Do this now:**
1. Build and deploy a **personal portfolio site**: a short intro, your best 3 to 4 projects with screenshots
   and live links, and how to contact you.
2. Polish your **GitHub profile**: a profile README, **pinned repositories** (your 4 projects + capstone),
   each with a clear README, screenshots and a live link.
3. Write a **professional headline** for LinkedIn or similar, like "Front-end developer: HTML, CSS,
   JavaScript, React", with a short, honest "About".

**You've got it when:** a stranger can land on your profile and, in under a minute, see five finished,
deployed projects. Quality over quantity: four finished projects beat ten half-built ones.

## Try it 4 - Find work safely

**Why it matters:** new developers in Nigeria are actively targeted by scams. Knowing the patterns protects
you.

**Do this now:**
1. Make a shortlist of where you'll look: **Upwork**, **Fiverr**, **LinkedIn**, **local referrals** (often
   the best when starting), and **remote job boards**.
2. Memorise the red flags below.

**You've got it when:** you can name three common scams and how to avoid each.

> **Warning, avoid scams:**
> - **Never pay to get a job.** Real employers and clients don't charge you to work.
> - **Overpayment/cheque scams:** a "client" overpays and asks you to refund the difference, the original
>   payment is fake. Never refund from your own money.
> - **"Trial projects" that are real work:** a free "test" that is actually a finished deliverable for their
>   business. A tiny, clearly-scoped sample is fine; building their real site for free is not.
> - **Verify** who you're dealing with, use the platform's payment protection, and get a deposit for direct
>   clients.

## Try it 5 - Rehearse your interview answers

**Why it matters:** interviews reward people who can explain their work out loud, not just build it.

**Do this now:**
1. Out loud, explain one of your projects: the problem, your approach, the tech, a challenge, what you'd
   improve.
2. Practise a small live-coding warm-up (a FizzBuzz-style task, and a DOM or React task). Think aloud.
3. Rehearse answering "I haven't used that yet, but here's how I'd find out", honesty plus a learning plan
   beats bluffing.

**You've got it when:** you can talk through a project confidently for two minutes without notes.

## What next (optional further learning)

Sensible next steps after this course:

- **TypeScript**, typed JavaScript, widely used in jobs.
- **Node.js / Express**, build your own backend.
- **Testing**, Vitest / React Testing Library.
- **Next.js**, a popular React framework.
- **Deeper accessibility and performance.**

Keep building, keep shipping, keep applying. Consistency is what turns "learning to code" into working as a
developer.

## Facilitator Notes

Hold the honest-outcomes line: celebrate the real, deployed portfolio and the habit of shipping, and be
clear that job-readiness continues beyond the course. For the capstone, insist on a written brief even for
case studies, scoping is a core professional skill. Make the scam-awareness section non-negotiable; new
developers in Nigeria are actively targeted. Keep any model capstone or solution code in `_facilitator/`.
End on momentum: every student should leave with a concrete "first three places I'll apply" list.
