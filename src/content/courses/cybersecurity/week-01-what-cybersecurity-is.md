**Goal:** By the end of this week you will have mapped the threats you actually face, checked whether your own
accounts have been caught in a breach, and ranked what matters most to protect. You learn this by investigating
your own life, not by reading theory.

## What you'll be able to do

- Explain cybersecurity in plain words, after seeing the threats for yourself
- Name the three things security protects (secret, correct, available) with real examples
- Spot how automated, money-driven attacks pick easy targets - not famous ones
- Build an honest risk list of your most valuable accounts and data

## Before you start (about 10 minutes)

- Open a blank document for your threat map and risk list.
- Warm-up: list the accounts and data that would hurt most if you lost them - email, mobile money, photos, any
  client data. Keep this list open; it becomes your build.

## Try it 1 - Check if you have already been breached

**Why it matters:** attackers do not hunt you personally. They buy huge lists of leaked passwords and try them
against millions of accounts automatically. Seeing your own data in one makes this real.

**Do this now:**
1. Go to a reputable breach-checking site (search "have i been pwned") and enter your main email address.
2. Note how many breaches it appears in, and which services leaked.
3. For any site where you reused that password, write it down - those are open doors you will close in Week 2.

**You've got it when:** you can say whether your email has been in a breach, and name at least one account you
need to fix.

## Try it 2 - Map the threats that actually reach you

**Why it matters:** you do not need to fear everything. A handful of threats cause most real harm, and most of
them target people, not machines.

**Do this now - in your document, list a real local example for each:**
1. **Phishing / scams** - e.g. a fake "your account is locked" SMS
2. **Account takeover** - e.g. a hijacked WhatsApp asking your contacts for airtime
3. **Malware** - e.g. a "cracked" app that steals or locks data
4. **Social engineering** - e.g. a "boss" on a new number urgently asking you to send money

**You've got it when:** your threat map has a real, local example beside each threat.

## Try it 3 - See who is really behind it (and why)

**Why it matters:** the attacker is rarely a hooded genius. It is usually a scammer after quick money, using
automated tools and casting a wide net for easy targets.

**Do this now:**
1. Search "common online scams Nigeria" and read one recent, reputable article.
2. Write down the single motive behind almost every scam you see. (Hint: it is money.)
3. Write one sentence on why "I'm not important enough to be targeted" is a dangerous belief.

**You've got it when:** you can explain that attackers hit easy targets, not famous ones - so basic protection
works.

> **Confidence is not safety.** The belief "it won't happen to me" is exactly what automated attacks rely on.

## Try it 4 - Rank your own risk honestly

**Why it matters:** security is about protecting what matters most to you, not everything equally.

**Do this now - for your top accounts and data:**
1. Write what would hurt most to lose or have stolen.
2. Beside each, note its current protection (strong password? 2FA? backed up?).
3. Note the easiest way an attacker could reach it (a reused password, no 2FA, a clickable scam).

**You've got it when:** you can point to the single weakest, most valuable thing you own online.

## Make it yours - the three goals of security

Keep this simple idea in mind and security stops feeling mysterious. Security tries to keep your information:

- **Secret (confidential):** only the right people can see it
- **Correct (integrity):** it is not changed or faked without permission
- **Available:** you can get to it when you need it

Almost every real harm - a hacked account, a scam payment, lost photos - is one of these three breaking.

### Watch out for

- "I'm not a target" (attackers hit easy targets, not famous ones)
- Protecting everything equally instead of what matters most
- Focusing only on technology and ignoring the human tricks
- Believing any system can be made perfectly safe
- Treating security as a one-time setup rather than a habit

## This week's build: your personal threat map and risk list

Make a short, useful document you could hand to a friend to help them get safer:

1. **In your own words:** what cybersecurity is and its three goals (3 or 4 sentences)
2. **Your threat map:** the main threats you face, each with a real local example
3. **Your risk list:** your most valuable accounts/data, their current protection, and the easiest attack path
4. **Your top three priorities** to fix first, with why

*Tip: the breach result from Try it 1 probably decides at least one of your top three priorities.*

- Check a second email or an old account on the breach site; note what leaked.

## Mentoring Session Plan (90 minutes)

| Time | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0-10 min | Welcome; the ethics rules; "defence only" |
| 10-30 min | What cybersecurity is; the three goals with real examples |
| 30-55 min | Threat map together; share local scams and takeovers |
| 55-75 min | Risk lists: each learner names their top assets and weak points |
| 75-90 min | Agree top priorities; brief for Week 2 |

## Facilitator Notes

Set the tone: practical, local, and defensive. Use real regional examples (mobile-money scams, WhatsApp account
takeovers, SIM-swap). The breach-check exercise lands the "automated, money-driven" point better than any
lecture, prepare to help learners who find alarming results stay calm and act. Counter the "I'm not a target"
belief directly. Keep it from feeling hopeless; basic habits defeat most attacks. Reinforce the ethics rules
from setup. Keep every example about protection; never demonstrate attacks.

## Can you do these now?

1. What are the three goals of security, and what does each protect?
2. Who is usually behind everyday attacks, and what do they want?
3. Why is "I'm not important enough to be targeted" a dangerous belief?
4. How do you decide what to protect first?
