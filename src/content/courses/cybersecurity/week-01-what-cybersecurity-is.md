**Goal:** Build a clear, honest picture of what cybersecurity is, the real threats and who is behind them,
and where your own biggest risks are, so you can protect what matters most.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this week you can:

- Explain what cybersecurity is in plain words
- Describe the three goals of security (keep data secret, correct, and available)
- Name the common threats and who is typically behind them
- Explain why ordinary people and small businesses are targets
- Make an honest list of your own biggest risks

## Lesson 1: What cybersecurity really is

**Cybersecurity** means protecting your accounts, devices, data, and online life from harm, theft, and misuse.
It is not only for big companies or experts. If you have an email, a phone, mobile money, or a social account,
you have something worth protecting, and something others may try to take.

At its heart, security tries to keep your information three things:

- **Secret (confidential):** only the right people can see it
- **Correct (integrity):** it is not changed or faked without permission
- **Available:** you can get to it when you need it

Most real-world harm, a hacked account, a scam payment, lost photos, is one of these three breaking. Keep
that simple idea in mind and security stops feeling mysterious.

> **Note:** This course is about **defence**: protecting yourself and others. It never teaches how to attack,
> because good security is about trust, and because attacking others is harmful and usually illegal.

## Lesson 2: The common threats

You do not need to fear everything. A handful of threats cause most real harm:

| Threat | What it is | Everyday example |
|---|---|---|
| Phishing / scams | Tricking you into giving secrets or money | A fake "your account is locked" message |
| Account takeover | Someone gets into your account | A reused password leaks and your email is hijacked |
| Malware | Harmful software on your device | A dodgy app or attachment that steals or locks data |
| Ransomware | Malware that locks your files for payment | Files encrypted, a ransom demanded |
| Data loss | Losing data to theft, damage, or deletion | A stolen phone with no backup |
| Social engineering | Manipulating people, not machines | A "boss" urgently asking you to send money |

Notice how many target **people**, not just technology. That is good news: your habits are your strongest
defence.

## Lesson 3: Who is behind attacks (and why)

Attackers are usually not the hooded genius of films. Most are:

- **Scammers** after quick money (the biggest everyday threat)
- **Opportunists** using automated tools that try millions of leaked passwords
- **Organised crime** running scams and ransomware at scale

Their motive is almost always **money**, and they prefer **easy targets**. They are not hunting you
personally; they cast wide nets and catch whoever is unprotected. This is why basic protection works so well:
you do not need to be unbeatable, just not the easiest target.

## Lesson 4: Why ordinary people are targets

A common, dangerous belief is "I'm not important enough to be targeted." But attackers want:

- Your **money** and mobile-money access
- Your **accounts** (to scam your contacts, or resell)
- Your **data** (to impersonate you or unlock other accounts)
- Your **device** (to use or hold to ransom)

Small businesses are especially targeted: they have money and data, but often weaker protection than big
firms. If you handle clients' data (as a VA, freelancer, or small business), protecting it is part of your
job and your reputation.

## Lesson 5: Assess your own risk honestly

Security is about protecting **what matters most to you**. Make a simple risk list:

1. **What would hurt most to lose or have stolen?** (email, money, photos, client data)
2. **How protected is each right now?** (strong password? 2FA? backed up?)
3. **What is the easiest way an attacker could reach it?** (a reused password, no 2FA, a clickable scam)

This tells you where to focus first. You will act on it over the coming weeks.

### Common mistakes

- "I'm not a target" (attackers hit easy targets, not famous ones)
- Protecting everything equally instead of what matters most
- Focusing only on technology and ignoring the human tricks
- Believing any system can be made perfectly safe
- Thinking security is a one-time setup rather than a habit

## Practice Exercises

1. Write the three goals of security in your own words, with an example of each breaking.
2. List five threats from Lesson 2 and a real local example of each.
3. Write down three accounts or data that would hurt most to lose.
4. For each, note its current protection (password strength, 2FA, backup).
5. Identify the single easiest way an attacker could reach one of them.
6. Explain, in two sentences, why a small business is an attractive target.
7. Start the security checklist; tick what you already do.

## Weekly Assignment (Due Before Week 2)

Create a **personal threat map and risk list**:

1. A short, plain explanation of **what cybersecurity is** and its three goals
2. A **threat map**: the main threats you face, with a local example of each
3. A **risk list**: your most valuable accounts/data, their current protection, and the easiest attack path
4. Your **top three priorities** to fix first, with why

## Facilitator Notes

Set the tone: practical, local, and defensive. Use real regional examples (mobile-money scams, WhatsApp
account takeovers, SIM-swap). Counter the "I'm not a target" belief directly with how automated, money-driven
attacks work. Keep it from feeling hopeless, basic habits defeat most attacks. Reinforce the ethics rules
from setup. Keep all examples about protection; never demonstrate attacks.

## Self-Check Questions

1. What are the three goals of security, and what does each protect?
2. Who is usually behind everyday attacks, and what do they want?
3. Why is "I'm not important enough to be targeted" a dangerous belief?
4. How do you decide what to protect first?
