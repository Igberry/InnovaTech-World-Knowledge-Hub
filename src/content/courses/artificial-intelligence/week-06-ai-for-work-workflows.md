**Goal:** Use AI on real, everyday work tasks and turn one repeatable task into a documented workflow, with a
human clearly in charge of judgement and checks.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this week you can:

- Apply AI to common work tasks (spreadsheets, planning, drafting, light coding help)
- Break a task into steps and decide where AI helps and where a human must check
- Document a repeatable AI-assisted workflow others could follow
- Judge where AI saves time and where it adds risk
- See how a workflow could later be automated (bridge to the Automation course)

## Lesson 1: AI on everyday work tasks

Beyond writing, AI helps with many day-to-day tasks. A few examples:

- **Spreadsheets:** "Explain how to write a formula that adds up column B where column A says 'Paid'." Then you
  test it on your data.
- **Planning:** turn a goal into a step-by-step plan or a checklist.
- **Drafting structures:** outlines, agendas, templates, simple scripts for a message.
- **Light coding help:** explaining a small snippet, or suggesting one, which you then test.

In every case, AI gives you a fast first version, and **you test and confirm** it works on your real task.

> **Tip:** For spreadsheet or code help, always test on a small, safe copy first. A formula that looks right can
> still do the wrong thing on your actual data.

## Lesson 2: Break the task into steps

The key skill is seeing a task as **steps**, then deciding, for each step, whether AI helps and where a human
must check. Take "prepare the weekly update from our sales notes":

| Step | AI helps? | Human check |
|---|---|---|
| Gather the week's notes | No (you do this) | - |
| Summarise the notes | Yes | Check against the notes for accuracy |
| Draft the update message | Yes | Edit into our voice; verify any numbers |
| Decide what to highlight | No (judgement) | You decide |
| Send it | No | You send |

Seeing the steps shows you exactly where AI saves time and where your judgement is non-negotiable.

## Lesson 3: Document a workflow

A **workflow** is a repeatable series of steps to get a job done. Documenting one (using the
[workflow outline](/downloads/artificial-intelligence/ai-workflow-outline.md)) means you, or someone else, can
do it the same good way every time. A good workflow document has:

1. The job it does and how often
2. The steps, with which use AI and which a human checks
3. The exact prompts for the AI steps
4. The quality checks before the result is "done"

Writing it down turns a clever one-off into a reliable, reusable process, and makes your AI use visible and
checkable.

## Lesson 4: Keep a human in charge

The safest and best AI workflows keep a person responsible for judgement and final checks. AI does the fast
first-draft work; you decide what is good enough and catch what is wrong. Build the checks into the workflow so
they always happen:

- Verify facts, numbers, and links (fact-check routine)
- Confirm no private or confidential data was used
- Read the result for sense, tone, and fit
- Add disclosure if expected

> **Important:** "The AI did it" is never an excuse for a mistake that goes out under your name. If you ship it,
> you own it. The checks are part of the job, not optional extras.

## Lesson 5: From workflow to automation (a bridge)

Once a workflow is clear and repeatable, parts of it can sometimes be **automated**, set up to run with less
manual effort. That is the subject of the InnovaTech [Automation](/courses/) path. For now, notice which of your
steps are so repetitive and rule-based that automating them later would help. Documenting the workflow is the
first step toward that.

### Common mistakes

- Using AI output (a formula, a snippet) without testing it on real data
- Not separating steps, so AI is trusted where human judgement was needed
- Skipping the built-in checks when busy
- Treating a one-off clever result as if it were a reliable process
- Putting confidential data into a tool to "speed up" a work task

## Practice Exercises

1. Use AI to help with one spreadsheet task; test it on a small copy and confirm it works.
2. Take a real task and break it into steps; mark where AI helps and where a human checks.
3. Ask AI to draft a checklist or plan for a task; refine it to fit your real situation.
4. Get AI to explain a small piece of code or a formula; verify the explanation.
5. Write the exact prompts you would reuse for one task, and save them to the prompt log.
6. Fill in the workflow outline for one repeatable task.
7. Identify one step in your workflow that could later be automated, and say why.

## Weekly Assignment (Due Before Week 7)

Document one **AI-assisted workflow** for a real, repeatable task:

1. A completed **workflow outline**: the job, the steps, which use AI, and the human checks
2. The **exact prompts** for the AI steps, saved and reusable
3. One **worked run** of the workflow, showing the result and the checks you did
4. A note on which step could be **automated later**, and why

### Grading guide

| Criteria | Weight |
|---|---|
| Workflow is clear, with steps and human checks well placed | 35% |
| Prompts are specific and reusable | 20% |
| A real run shows the workflow producing a checked result | 30% |
| Thoughtful note on future automation | 15% |

### Definition of done

- [ ] Workflow outline completed
- [ ] Reusable prompts included
- [ ] One real run with its result and checks
- [ ] An automation candidate identified with a reason

## Facilitator Notes

The two ideas to land: break tasks into steps, and keep a human in charge of checks. The step table is the core
exercise; do it live with a task learners recognise. Insist that AI-suggested formulas or code are tested on
safe sample data before use. Use this week to tee up the Automation course by naming which steps are automatable.
Keep reinforcing privacy and fact-checking. Keep tasks realistic and local.
