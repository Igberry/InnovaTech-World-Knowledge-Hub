**Goal:** Use AI to research topics, learn new things faster, and organise your work, while applying a
reliable fact-checking routine so nothing made-up slips through.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this week you can:

- Use AI to research a topic and get a useful starting overview
- Apply a step-by-step routine to verify AI output
- Use AI as a study aid (explanations, practice questions, plans)
- Organise messy information into clear structures with AI
- Judge when AI research is enough and when to go to primary sources

## Lesson 1: AI as a research starting point

AI is a fast way to get **oriented** on a topic: a plain overview, the key terms, and the main questions to
ask. That is genuinely valuable; it can save hours of confusion.

But remember Week 1: AI predicts plausible words, so its "facts" may be wrong, out of date, or invented. So the
rule is simple and firm: **use AI to start your research, never to finish it.** The overview points you where
to look; real sources confirm what is true.

> **Note:** Some AI tools can search the web and show sources. That helps, but you still check the sources
> actually say what the AI claims. A cited source is only useful if it is real and relevant.

## Lesson 2: The fact-check routine

For anything that matters, run this quick routine (the worksheet walks through it):

1. **Spot the risky bits:** facts, numbers, names, quotes, links, laws, recent events.
2. **Find an independent source:** search for the claim on a trusted, official, or well-known site.
3. **Check links and quotes directly:** open the link; confirm the quote exists word for word.
4. **Decide:** confirmed (use it), unconfirmed (do not present as fact), or wrong (correct or remove).

This takes a few minutes and saves you from passing on false information, which can damage your work and your
reputation.

> **Warning:** "The AI said so" is never a source. The source is the real, checkable place the fact comes from.
> If it does not exist, treat the claim as unproven.

## Lesson 3: AI as a study aid

AI is a patient tutor. Used honestly, it helps you learn faster:

- "Explain [topic] simply, with one example."
- "Give me five practice questions on [topic], then check my answers."
- "I think [X]. Is that right? Where am I going wrong?"
- "Make a one-week study plan for [goal], 30 minutes a day."

The honest line: use AI to **understand and practise**, not to produce work you pass off as your own where
that would be cheating (an exam, graded homework, a client deliverable you claim is all yours).

> **Important:** Learning with AI only works if you still do the thinking. If you copy answers without
> understanding, you learn nothing and the gaps show later. Let AI explain; you do the practice.

## Lesson 4: Organising and productivity

AI is great at turning mess into order:

- Turn rough notes into a clear outline or table
- Group a long list into sensible categories
- Draft an agenda, a plan, or a checklist from a brain-dump
- Rewrite a tangled paragraph as clear steps

These tasks are low-risk (you can see if the result is right) and save real time. Combine them with your own
judgement about what matters.

## Lesson 5: When AI is not enough

Go to primary or expert sources, not AI, when:

- The topic is **high-stakes** (health, money, legal, safety)
- You need **current, exact** facts (prices, laws, recent events)
- The answer must be **authoritative** (official rules, specifications)

AI can still help you prepare good questions for those sources. It just should not be the final word.

### Common mistakes

- Treating an AI overview as finished research
- Trusting AI-provided sources without opening them
- Using AI to produce work dishonestly instead of to learn
- Skipping the fact-check on "small" claims that turn out to matter
- Relying on AI for high-stakes or very recent information

## Practice Exercises

1. Ask AI for an overview of a topic, then list the claims you would need to verify.
2. Run the full fact-check routine on one claim; record your sources and verdict.
3. Open every link an AI gives in one answer; note which were real and relevant.
4. Use AI to make five practice questions on something you are learning; answer them yourself.
5. Turn a page of messy notes into a clear outline with AI; check nothing was invented.
6. Ask AI to make a one-week study plan; adjust it to your real life.
7. Pick a high-stakes question and list the primary sources you would check instead of AI.

## Weekly Assignment (Due Before Week 6)

Produce a **verified research summary** on a topic of your choice:

1. A **one-page summary** of the topic, started with AI and written in your own words
2. A **fact-check log**: at least **three claims** you verified, each with the source and verdict
3. One claim you found to be **wrong or unverifiable**, and how you handled it
4. A short note on **where you would go beyond AI** for this topic, and why

### Grading guide

| Criteria | Weight |
|---|---|
| Summary is clear, useful, and in your own words | 25% |
| Three or more claims genuinely verified with real sources | 35% |
| A wrong/unverifiable claim caught and handled honestly | 20% |
| Sound judgement on AI's limits for the topic | 20% |

### Definition of done

- [ ] One-page summary in your own words
- [ ] At least three claims with sources and verdicts
- [ ] One caught error or unverifiable claim, handled
- [ ] A note on where primary sources are needed

## Facilitator Notes

Fact-checking is the core skill this week and a highlight of the whole course. Run a live verification on an AI
answer that contains a plausible but false claim or a fake source; the moment learners catch it is the lesson.
Stress honest study use (AI to learn, not to cheat) and the high-stakes exceptions. Make sure learners actually
open links rather than trusting citations. Keep topics local and practical.

## Self-Check Questions

1. Why should AI be the start of research, not the end?
2. What are the four steps of the fact-check routine?
3. How do you use AI to learn without it becoming cheating?
4. Name two situations where you must go beyond AI to a primary source.
