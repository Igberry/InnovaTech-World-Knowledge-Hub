**Goal:** Organise routine messages and meetings, capture useful notes, and bring the course artifacts together in one clearly labelled practice pack.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this week, you can:

- Organise, search and write routine Outlook messages with safe attachment and recipient habits.
- Create a calendar meeting with a clear time zone, agenda and authorised participants.
- Use Teams chat, channels, meetings, files, notifications and status appropriately.
- Organise OneNote notebooks, sections, pages, tags and meeting notes.
- Connect an email to a task and a meeting to an action list.
- Assemble and review an Office Admin Pack and describe Office skills honestly on a CV.

## Tools / Accounts Needed This Week

Use Outlook, Teams and OneNote where your personal, school or work account provides them. Features vary by license, region, account type and app version. A licensed desktop app is not required for every exercise; use the templates to practise the workflow offline if an app is unavailable. Use only a test meeting or an authorised participant. Do not invite real people or store real client information in the capstone.

## Lessons

### Lesson 1: Outlook email and professional etiquette

Email is useful for messages that need a clear record. Start with a specific subject such as **“Practice menu review — response requested by Friday”** rather than “Hello” or “Important”. Put the request or main point near the top, include only necessary context, and close politely. Reread the message as the recipient would.

Use folders to organise messages, **categories** to label them across views, **flags** to mark follow-up, and **rules** to sort predictable messages where supported. Search is often faster than creating many nested folders. A signature should contain only approved professional details. An out-of-office response should state when you expect to return and identify an approved alternative contact only if the organisation permits it.

**Reply All** sends your response to everyone on the thread; use it only when each person needs the answer. Use **Cc** for people who need visibility and **Bcc** carefully, such as a permitted distribution where recipients should not see each other's addresses. Do not use Bcc to hide a message from someone who should know who received it. Check recipients before sending.

An attachment sends a copy of a file. A link can point to a shared copy that may update; it also depends on the recipient's permission. Choose the method that fits the purpose, then check the file and access settings. For a large or sensitive file, follow the organisation's approved sharing process. Do not include passwords, verification codes or unnecessary personal data in email.

Scheduled send and out-of-office controls may vary across Outlook versions. If a scheduled message is used, check the send time zone and confirm the message is in the right folder. A scheduled send is not a guarantee of delivery or recipient reading.

### Lesson 2: Calendar, invitations, contacts and tasks

Use a descriptive event title and include the purpose, date, start and end time, and time zone. A calendar can display local time or another selected zone; verify the invite after saving, especially when participants are in different locations. The **Scheduling Assistant** or availability view may help compare calendars when the account and permissions support it. Do not assume a colleague's availability is visible.

Shared calendars should be shared only under an approved policy. An invitation creates a notification for its recipients, so do not send test invitations to `.invalid` addresses or real people without consent. For class practice, prepare an unsent draft or use an authorised test account/participant.

Contacts store information about people; tasks capture an action to complete. Write an action as a verb plus an outcome, such as “Confirm the practice menu list,” then set a realistic due date and owner. Avoid storing sensitive notes in a contact card or task that could be shared broadly. Calendar events, tasks and contacts can connect, but review where each item is saved.

### Lesson 3: Teams chats, channels and meetings

Teams may provide one-to-one or group chats, channels for project conversations, meetings, shared files, presence status and notifications. Use a channel for a topic that a team needs to find later; use a direct message for a brief, appropriate conversation. Avoid posting private or sensitive information in a group chat just because it is convenient.

Before a meeting, check the participant list, time zone, agenda and file permissions. During a call, mute when not speaking, use chat and reactions respectfully, and confirm whether recording or transcription is enabled. Recording requires a lawful and approved reason and participant awareness; it is not part of this course. After a meeting, share only approved notes and actions.

> **Privacy:** Do not record a class meeting, share an attendee list or send a practice invitation to a real person without their consent and the organisation's approval.

Manage notifications so you can focus without missing genuinely important work. Set your presence accurately and follow your organisation's norms. If Teams is unavailable to a personal account or a learner has limited data, use the agenda and minutes templates to simulate the same process offline.

### Lesson 4: OneNote notebooks, sections and pages

OneNote organises information in **notebooks**, **sections** and **pages**. A notebook may hold a course or project; sections can group meetings, planning and reference material; pages capture one topic or meeting. Use page titles that can be found with search. Tags such as **To Do**, **Question** or **Important** can help, but tag meaning should be consistent.

Use the supplied minutes template to record the date, purpose, discussion, decisions and action owners. Separate facts from questions. Do not write a long transcript when a concise record is enough. Linking a OneNote page to a meeting can make follow-up easier, but sharing permissions still apply. Keep the notebook in an authorised location and do not place passwords or unnecessary personal information in it.

### Lesson 5: Using the apps together

A simple workflow might look like this: an Outlook email asks for a budget review; you create a task with an owner and due date; you schedule a meeting using an approved calendar; you prepare an agenda in OneNote; and after the meeting you record decisions and actions in the same project notebook. The spreadsheet and presentation remain in their work folders, with permission-controlled links shared when needed.

The tools do not replace a clear process. If each item is saved in a different place, a team may lose track of decisions. Agree on a folder, naming rule and source of truth. For the Fresh Bites practice case, keep the starter files, edited copies and exports separate. Use the Office standards guide to record the routine.

### Lesson 6: Mailbox and account security

Phishing messages may use urgency, unexpected invoices, fake sign-in pages or attachments that ask you to enable content. Hovering over a link or checking the sender may help, but neither proves a message is safe. When in doubt, navigate to the official service independently or confirm through a known, approved channel.

Do not open an unexpected attachment, share a verification code, approve an unrequested sign-in prompt or enter credentials from an email link. Report suspicious messages according to the organisation's process. Use strong, unique passwords and two-step verification where available. Review forwarding rules and connected apps if an account behaves unexpectedly. The [Cybersecurity Foundations course, Week 4](/courses/cybersecurity/week-04-device-data-safety/) covers device and data safety in more depth.

### Lesson 7: Office skills and career communication

Office tools appear in administrative assistant, data-entry, virtual-assistant and front-desk roles, among many others. A job title alone does not establish that a person is qualified. Describe the work you can demonstrate: for example, “formatted a multi-section practice proposal with styles and an automatic table of contents” or “built a fictional inventory tracker with a low-stock rule.”

On a CV, list only tools and skills you can explain. Separate training exercises from paid or volunteer work. Do not claim a Microsoft certification unless you earned that specific credential. Do not include a client's name or confidential document in a portfolio without permission. A well-organised practice pack can show learning, but it does not guarantee an interview, job or income.

### Lesson 8: Optional Microsoft certification overview

Microsoft Learn's official pages were checked on **12 November 2026**. They describe the **Microsoft Office Specialist: Associate (Microsoft 365 Apps)** pathway as requiring three qualifying exams in different programs, including at least one Microsoft 365 Apps exam. Current examples listed on the Associate page are Word **MO-110**, Excel **MO-210** and PowerPoint **MO-310**; the page also lists Office 2019 exam options. Each exam page says exam price depends on the country or region where the exam is proctored. The Word and Excel pages recommend about 150 hours of instruction and hands-on experience for the particular credential.

This course is a practical introduction and does not claim to meet that experience guidance or prepare a learner to pass an exam. The certification is optional and separate from the InnovaTech course completion record. We did not verify a Nigeria-specific test-centre schedule, current local fee or every available language. Check the current Microsoft Learn page and its linked exam provider information before planning or paying. Do not purchase an exam or subscription because of a course example.

## Common Mistakes

- Writing an unclear subject line or replying to every recipient by default.
- Attaching a file when the recipient needs a permission-controlled shared copy.
- Scheduling an event without checking its time zone or attendee list.
- Treating Teams status as proof that a person is available.
- Recording meeting notes as an unfiltered transcript.
- Putting a password, verification code or private customer details in OneNote.
- Treating an optional credential path as included, guaranteed or required for this course.
- Claiming a fictional portfolio exercise as paid work or an earned certification.

## Practice Exercises

1. Draft a concise Outlook message with a specific subject, one clear request and a suitable closing; do not send it.
2. Create a practice email rule or category, then locate the message using search.
3. Make an unsent calendar meeting draft with a date, time zone, agenda link and authorised test attendee or no attendee.
4. Create a OneNote notebook section and page from the minutes template; add two actions with owners.
5. Practise writing the same update as an email, a short Teams message and a meeting action.
6. Review a simulated suspicious message and list the checks you would make without opening its attachment.
7. Describe two Office tasks honestly on a CV using evidence from the course artifacts.
8. Review the optional certification page and note one requirement and one detail you would verify before registering.

## Weekly Assignment (Capstone — due at course completion)

Submit the **Office Admin Pack** for the fictional Fresh Bites case:

1. A business letter and a mail merge review copy for the ten fictional records; do not send the merge.
2. A budget and sales workbook with a chart and PivotTable.
3. A ten-slide pitch deck with a one-minute talk.
4. A scheduled team meeting with an agenda in Outlook and minutes in OneNote. Use a draft or an authorised practice participant; do not invite real people without consent.
5. A one-page Office standards guide covering naming, folders, templates and sharing.

**Rubric:** accuracy 25%, professional design 25%, consistency 20%, use of the right tool 15%, organisation 15%.

**Definition of done**

- [ ] All five artifacts are present, open and use the shared naming convention.
- [ ] Mail merge uses fictional data, is previewed and is not sent.
- [ ] Spreadsheet formulas, chart and PivotTable are checked against the source.
- [ ] The deck has speaker notes, a rehearsed one-minute talk and clear fictional labels.
- [ ] The Outlook/OneNote meeting has a time zone, agenda, decisions and actions without private data.
- [ ] The standards guide is one page and states safe file and sharing routines.
- [ ] Screenshots or exports expose no private account details or unrelated files.
