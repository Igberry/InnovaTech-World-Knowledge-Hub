**Goal:** Connect email, calendar, meetings and notes into one smooth routine, then pull every artifact from the course into a single Office Admin Pack you can show. You finish by doing - drafting, organising and assembling real deliverables.

## What you'll be able to do

- Write and organise routine Outlook email with safe recipient and attachment habits
- Create a calendar meeting with a clear time zone, agenda and authorised attendees
- Use Teams chat, channels, meetings and status appropriately
- Organise OneNote notebooks, sections, pages and meeting notes
- Connect an email to a task and a meeting to an action list
- Assemble your Office Admin Pack and describe your Office skills honestly on a CV

## Before you start (about 10 minutes)

- Open the capstone brief, the agenda template and the minutes template.
- Warm-up: write one email subject line that states the action and the deadline, such as **"Practice menu review - response requested by Friday"**. A good subject is half a good email.
- List which artifacts you already have from Weeks 1 to 5 and what still needs a polish.
- Optional 10-minute video: search YouTube for **"Kevin Stratvert Outlook email rules calendar meeting"**.

## Try it 1 - Write email people can act on

**Why it matters:** a clear subject and a request near the top gets a faster reply than a wall of text.

**Do this now:**
1. Draft a message with a specific subject, the main request in the first line, and a polite close. Do not send it.
2. Decide between **Reply All** (everyone needs it), **Cc** (visibility) and **Bcc** (recipients should not see each other). Check recipients before sending.
3. Choose an attachment versus a shared link on purpose, and never put a password or verification code in email.

**You've got it when:** a stranger could read your subject line and know what you need and by when.

## Try it 2 - Organise the inbox with search, rules and flags

**Why it matters:** search and a few rules beat dozens of nested folders.

**Do this now:**
1. Create one rule or category to sort a predictable message.
2. Add a flag for follow-up on a message.
3. Find a message with search rather than clicking through folders.

**You've got it when:** you can locate any message in seconds without a deep folder tree.

## Try it 3 - Set up a meeting that runs itself

**Why it matters:** a clear title, time zone and agenda is the difference between a useful meeting and a confused one.

**Do this now:**
1. Create an unsent calendar meeting draft with a descriptive title, purpose, start and end time, and time zone.
2. Verify the time zone after saving, especially for attendees elsewhere.
3. Use a draft or an authorised test participant - do not send invitations to real people or `.invalid` addresses.

**You've got it when:** your meeting draft states purpose, time zone and agenda, with no real person invited.

## Try it 4 - Use Teams the right way

**Why it matters:** the right channel versus chat keeps a team able to find things later.

**Do this now:**
1. Decide what belongs in a channel (findable, team-wide) versus a direct message (brief, personal).
2. Before a practice call, check participants, time zone, agenda and file permissions.
3. Set your presence accurately and tune notifications so you can focus.

> **Privacy:** Do not record a class meeting, share an attendee list or send a practice invitation to a real person without their consent and the organisation's approval.

**You've got it when:** you can say which updates go to a channel and which to a direct message, and why.

## Try it 5 - Capture notes in OneNote

**Why it matters:** notes that can be searched and acted on beat a long transcript nobody re-reads.

**Do this now:**
1. Create a notebook, a section and a page from the minutes template.
2. Record date, purpose, discussion, decisions and action owners. Separate facts from questions.
3. Add two actions with owners and due dates. Keep passwords and personal data out.

**You've got it when:** your notes show clear decisions and owned actions, not a transcript.

## Try it 6 - Make the apps work together

**Why it matters:** the tools only help if one process ties them together.

**Do this now:**
1. Walk one workflow end to end: an email asks for a budget review, you create a task with an owner and due date, you schedule a meeting, you prep the agenda in OneNote, and you record decisions after.
2. Write the same short update three ways: as an email, a Teams message and a meeting action.
3. Agree one folder, naming rule and source of truth, and record it in the Office standards guide.

**You've got it when:** you can trace a single request from email to task to meeting to recorded action.

## Try it 7 - Protect your mailbox and describe skills honestly

**Why it matters:** phishing targets inboxes, and honest CV claims build trust an inflated one destroys.

**Do this now:**
1. Review a simulated suspicious message and list the checks you would make without opening the attachment.
2. Turn on two-step verification and never approve an unrequested sign-in or share a code. The [Cybersecurity Foundations course, Week 4](/courses/cybersecurity/week-04-device-data-safety/) goes deeper.
3. Write two honest CV lines from your course artifacts, such as "formatted a multi-section practice proposal with styles and an automatic table of contents." This course builds job-ready skills; it does not guarantee a job, income or a certification.

**You've got it when:** you can spot a phishing message and describe your Office work truthfully, evidence first.

### Watch out for

- An unclear subject or replying to everyone by default
- Attaching a file when a permission-controlled shared copy is needed
- Scheduling without checking time zone or attendee list
- Treating a Teams status as proof someone is free
- Recording notes as an unfiltered transcript
- Putting a password or private customer detail in OneNote
- Claiming a fictional practice exercise as paid work or an earned certification

## This week's build: your Office Admin Pack (capstone)

Assemble the full pack for the fictional Fresh Bites case:

1. A business letter and a mail merge review copy for the ten fictional records (do not send the merge).
2. A budget and sales workbook with a chart and PivotTable.
3. A ten-slide pitch deck with a one-minute talk.
4. A scheduled team meeting with an agenda in Outlook and minutes in OneNote - use a draft or an authorised participant; do not invite real people without consent.
5. A one-page Office standards guide covering naming, folders, templates and sharing.

### Grading guide

| Criteria | Weight |
|---|---|
| Accuracy | 25% |
| Professional design | 25% |
| Consistency | 20% |
| Use of the right tool | 15% |
| Organisation | 15% |

### Definition of done

- [ ] All five artifacts are present, open and use the shared naming convention.
- [ ] Mail merge uses fictional data, is previewed and is not sent.
- [ ] Spreadsheet formulas, chart and PivotTable are checked against the source.
- [ ] The deck has speaker notes, a rehearsed one-minute talk and clear fictional labels.
- [ ] The Outlook/OneNote meeting has a time zone, agenda, decisions and actions without private data.
- [ ] The standards guide is one page and states safe file and sharing routines.
- [ ] Screenshots or exports expose no private account details or unrelated files.

## Optional: Microsoft certification at a glance

Microsoft Learn's pages (checked 12 November 2026) describe the **Microsoft Office Specialist: Associate (Microsoft 365 Apps)** pathway as three qualifying exams, including at least one Microsoft 365 Apps exam. Current examples are Word **MO-110**, Excel **MO-210** and PowerPoint **MO-310**; Office 2019 options are also listed. Exam price depends on the country where it is proctored, and the Word and Excel pages suggest about 150 hours of instruction and practice.

This course is a practical introduction and does not claim to meet that guidance or prepare you to pass an exam. The certification is optional and separate from your InnovaTech completion record. Check the current Microsoft Learn page and its exam provider before planning or paying. Do not buy an exam or subscription because of a course example.
