**Goal:** Build a short, consistent pitch deck and deliver a clear one-minute talk - by building and rehearsing, not by reading about slides. You walk away with a deck you can show and a talk you can give.

## What you'll be able to do

- Structure a pitch around problem, solution, evidence and ask
- Use Slide Master layouts and one consistent theme
- Choose the right mix of text, images, icons, tables and charts
- Make slides readable with contrast, white space and few words
- Use speaker notes and rehearse with a timer
- Share or export a deck safely and deliver a confident one-minute talk

## Before you start (about 10 minutes)

- Open the ten-slide starter content and the design checklist.
- Warm-up: write your pitch in one sentence - "Fresh Bites helps [who] do [what] so they can [benefit]." Everything else supports that line.
- Mark any content in the starter that is an assumption, a fictional amount or simulated feedback.
- Optional 10-minute video: search YouTube for **"Kevin Stratvert PowerPoint slide master layouts"**.

## Try it 1 - Write the story before you touch a slide

**Why it matters:** a deck is a story, not decoration. Outline first and the design is easy.

**Do this now:**
1. Write ten slide titles in order: title, problem, solution, process, package options, sample figures, labelled practice feedback, quality checks, next step, close.
2. Give each slide one idea. Use a message headline ("Confirm the count before preparation") instead of a label ("Slide 4").
3. If you cannot say what a slide is for, cut or split it.

**You've got it when:** your ten titles alone tell the whole story.

## Try it 2 - Set up the Slide Master and theme

**Why it matters:** the master sets fonts, colours and layout once, so every slide matches without per-slide fiddling.

**Do this now:**
1. Pick a simple theme and two or three complementary colours.
2. Set a clear heading and body font hierarchy in the Slide Master.
3. Change the master once and check that slides on different layouts still look right. Use a labelled placeholder, not a real logo, in the fictional deck.

**You've got it when:** your slides share one consistent look you did not set slide by slide.

## Try it 3 - Put content on slides that breathe

**Why it matters:** a wall of text loses the room. The speaker carries the detail; the slide supports it.

**Do this now:**
1. Turn one long paragraph into a headline plus a few concise bullets.
2. Add one image that supports a point (check usage rights, add alt text) or a labelled placeholder.
3. Use a table only where exact values need comparing, such as a fictional menu and sample amount - and label figures as fictional.

**You've got it when:** no slide makes a reader squint, and every slide has one clear message.

## Try it 4 - Add an Excel chart

**Why it matters:** a clean chart makes a trend obvious in a second.

**Do this now:**
1. Use a line chart for a trend or a bar/column chart for comparison - borrow the Week 4 sales workbook for data.
2. Add a title, axis labels and units; skip pie charts when categories are many or close.
3. Decide whether the chart is embedded, linked or pasted as an image, and state on the slide that the data is fictional.

**You've got it when:** your chart reads clearly and is labelled as fictional practice data.

## Try it 5 - Fix a busy slide (before and after)

**Why it matters:** seeing a bad slide become a good one teaches design faster than any rule list.

**Do this now:**
1. Take a crowded slide: long paragraph, three fonts, several bright colours, clip art, a tiny table.
2. Rebuild it: one message headline, three bullets, one aligned image or placeholder, consistent colours, a legible table with a "fictional practice figures" note.
3. Review in slide-show mode, not just the edit canvas, and hunt for clipped text and low contrast.

**You've got it when:** you can point to three specific improvements between your before and after.

## Try it 6 - Speaker notes and the one-minute talk

**Why it matters:** the talk is the real deliverable. Notes are prompts, not a script to read aloud.

**Do this now:**
1. Add brief speaker notes to three slides - reminders and transitions, not full sentences.
2. Rehearse a one-minute talk with a timer: state the problem, the response, and one next step.
3. If you run long, cut detail rather than talking faster. Ask a listener what they remembered.

**You've got it when:** you can deliver the core message in about a minute without reading the slide.

## Try it 7 - Export and share safely

**Why it matters:** the last check stops you sharing private notes or a broken layout.

**Do this now:**
1. Export to PDF and inspect it on a second screen or viewer for clipping and font issues.
2. If you practise in Teams, share the slide show or one window - not the full desktop - and close private tabs first.
3. Confirm the audience sees the slide, not your Presenter View notes. Do not invite real people without consent; use a test meeting.

**You've got it when:** your exported deck opens clean and exposes no private notes or windows.

### Watch out for

- Opening with company history instead of the audience's problem
- A paragraph on every slide with shrinking font
- Editing each slide by hand instead of using the master
- An unlicensed photo, or practice feedback shown as a real testimonial
- A chart with no units or no "fictional" label
- Rehearsing only in edit mode and missing slide-show clipping
- Sharing the full desktop or private speaker notes by mistake

> **Important:** The sample feedback in the supplied deck is invented for role-play. Do not publish it as a customer quote or present it as evidence of a real outcome.

## This week's build: a ten-slide pitch and a one-minute talk

Build a **ten-slide pitch deck** for the fictional Fresh Bites catering service with a problem, proposed service, sample menu packages, a clearly labelled fictional pricing table, a fictional practice-only social-proof sentence, an explicit next step and a contact placeholder. Use a Slide Master or consistent layouts, add speaker notes, and deliver or record a one-minute talk on one slide. Do not present the social-proof line as a real testimonial, and add no unapproved contact information.

### Grading guide

| Criteria | Weight |
|---|---|
| Structure and message | 30% |
| Design | 30% |
| Use of master and consistency | 20% |
| Delivery | 20% |

### Definition of done

- [ ] The deck has ten slides and follows a clear problem-to-next-step story.
- [ ] Layouts, fonts, colours and slide titles are consistent.
- [ ] Figures and the role-play sentence are visibly identified as fictional.
- [ ] Speaker notes are present; the one-minute talk has been rehearsed or recorded.
- [ ] The PDF or deck opens and shows no clipped content.
- [ ] No private notes, unrelated window or unapproved contact detail is shared.

## Extra quick wins (optional)

- Add one subtle, consistent transition and resist animating every bullet.
- Prepare an answer for one likely question the deck does not cover.
- Record your one-minute talk once and watch it back for filler words.

## Mentoring Session Plan (90 min)

| Time | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0-10 min | Review Week 4 chart and select one message for the pitch |
| 10-25 min | Build the story outline and ten slide titles |
| 25-40 min | Demo: Slide Master, layouts and consistent typography |
| 40-55 min | Improve one busy slide and add a chart with units |
| 55-68 min | Add speaker notes and rehearse the one-minute talk |
| 68-80 min | Export and inspect a PDF; discuss online sharing privacy |
| 80-88 min | Peer feedback on the message and delivery |
| 88-90 min | Confirm assignment submission and fictional-data labels |

## Facilitator Notes

Use only the supplied fictional content and amounts. Ask learners to show the master or a layout decision, not just the finished slides. Check that the sample feedback remains labelled as role-play. Do not record a learner or publish a presentation without informed permission. Offer an offline presentation rehearsal for learners with limited data or no Teams account.
