**Goal:** Build a short, consistent presentation that helps an audience understand a practice proposal and its next step.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this week, you can:

- Structure a short pitch around a problem, solution, evidence or example, and ask.
- Apply Slide Master layouts and a consistent theme.
- Choose appropriate text, images, icons, SmartArt, tables and charts.
- Improve slide readability using contrast, white space and concise wording.
- Use speaker notes, Presenter View and rehearsal timing.
- Share or export a deck safely and deliver a clear one-minute talk.

## Tools / Accounts Needed This Week

Use PowerPoint desktop or web if available; LibreOffice Impress can practise slide structure and design, though menus and compatibility differ. A screen and keyboard are useful. Use the supplied fictional deck content and design checklist. Teams is optional; do not add real recipients or display private information during screen sharing.

## Lessons

### Lesson 1: Structure a presentation before designing it

A useful pitch helps a specific audience understand a problem, a proposed response, why the response is worth considering, and what decision or next step is requested. The ten-slide practice deck follows that sequence: title, problem, solution, process, package options, sample figures, clearly labelled practice feedback, quality checks, next step and close.

Write a short outline before opening PowerPoint. Give each slide one main idea. A title like “Delivery Plan” is clearer than “Slide 4”; a message headline such as “Confirm the count before preparation” says even more. If the audience cannot tell what to remember from a slide, simplify or divide it.

### Lesson 2: Slide Master, layouts and themes

The **Slide Master** controls repeating design choices such as fonts, colours, logo placeholders and footer placement. Layouts define where a title, body, image or chart goes. Start with a simple theme, choose two or three complementary colours and set a consistent heading/body font hierarchy. Do not change each slide separately unless there is a good reason.

If you update the master, inspect slides that use different layouts; a change can shift placeholders or alter contrast. Do not place a real logo into the fictional Fresh Bites deck. If you have not been given permission to use an image or brand mark, use a labelled placeholder.

### Lesson 3: Text, images, icons, SmartArt and tables

Use short phrases rather than paragraphs. The speaker explains the detail; the slide supports it. A useful slide often has a headline and a few bullets, but the exact number depends on the message and audience. Avoid shrinking text merely to fit more words.

Use images to support a point, not as decoration. Check usage rights, keep a record of the source and add alternative text for informative visuals. Icons can label steps or categories; use them consistently. SmartArt can show a short process, but a plain four-step diagram may be easier to edit and read.

Use a table when the audience must compare exact values, such as a fictional menu and sample amount. Keep columns limited and use clear units. A table should not become a screenshot of a spreadsheet with tiny text. If the figures are hypothetical, label them on the slide and in speaker notes.

### Lesson 4: Use an Excel chart

For a slide showing a trend, use a line chart; for category comparison, use a bar or column chart. Copy a chart from Excel or create it within PowerPoint, then check whether the chart is embedded, linked or pasted as an image. Linked charts may not update if a source workbook moves; an image will not be editable. Choose intentionally.

Add a descriptive title, axis labels and units. Do not use a pie chart when there are many categories or values are close. Use the fictional sales workbook from Week 4 if you need practice data. State that the data is fictional; do not imply that a chart describes actual Fresh Bites performance.

### Lesson 5: Design rules and before/after review

Good design makes the content easier to understand. Use contrast between text and background, preserve white space, align objects, and use a consistent type scale. Keep text large enough for the back of a room or a phone screen. Avoid putting a sentence over a busy photo. Do not rely on colour alone to distinguish a warning or status.

**Before:** a slide with a long paragraph, three fonts, multiple bright colours, a clip-art image, and a tiny pricing table.  
**After:** one message headline, three concise bullets, one aligned image or labelled placeholder, consistent colours, and a legible table with an explicit “fictional practice figures” note.

Review the deck in slide-show mode, not only in the editing canvas. Check for clipped text, low contrast, crowded edges and inconsistent spacing.

### Lesson 6: Animations and transitions

Transitions change between slides; animations reveal or move objects on a slide. Use them only when they help sequence a process or focus attention. A subtle, consistent transition is usually enough. Excess motion can distract, create accessibility barriers or slow a presentation on an older device. Avoid animating every bullet. Test on the device you will use.

### Lesson 7: Speaker notes, Presenter View and rehearsal

Speaker notes are prompts for the presenter. Write brief reminders, examples and transitions rather than a full script that encourages reading word-for-word. Presenter View can show notes, the current slide and next slide on a presenter screen while the audience sees the slide show; it may require a second display and may not be available in the same form in a web app.

Rehearse the one-minute talk with a timer. State the problem, explain the proposed response and end with one next step. If you run long, remove secondary detail rather than speaking faster. Ask a listener what they understood and revise the wording.

### Lesson 8: Handouts and exporting to PDF or video

Use **Export** or **Save As** to create a PDF handout when an editable slide deck is not needed. Check fonts, notes, slide order and page count. A handout may use one or more slides per page; choose a layout that remains readable. Keep the source `.pptx` separately from the PDF.

Video export may be available only in some desktop versions and can produce a large file. Confirm storage and data limits before creating or uploading it. If video is required for practice, a live talk or a small screen recording may be more practical, but do not record other people without consent. Add captions if the app supports them and review their accuracy.

### Lesson 9: Presenting online in Teams

In an online meeting, share the slide show or a specific window rather than the full desktop when possible. Close private tabs and messages first. Check that the audience sees the slide, not Presenter View notes. Tell participants when you are sharing and stop sharing after the presentation.

If Teams access is unavailable, rehearse offline with a partner or record a self-review. Do not create a meeting invitation to real people for this course unless they have agreed to participate. Use a test meeting or an organisation-approved practice space.

### Lesson 10: Handling nerves and questions

Nerves are common. Prepare the opening sentence, know the main point of each slide, and rehearse the ending. Take a breath before answering. If a question is outside the information in the deck, say what you know, what you would verify and when you would follow up. Do not invent evidence or answer by making a claim stronger than the data supports.

For the fictional pitch, the “social proof” slide includes a sample role-play sentence. It must remain labelled as fictional. Never pass it off as an actual testimonial, customer outcome or independently verified result.

> **Important:** The sample feedback in the supplied deck is invented for role-play. Do not publish it as a customer quote or present it as evidence of a real outcome.

## Common Mistakes

- Opening with a long company history instead of the audience's problem.
- Writing a paragraph on every slide and shrinking the font to fit it.
- Changing each slide manually rather than using a master and layouts.
- Using an unlicensed photo or presenting practice feedback as a real testimonial.
- Showing a chart without units or without saying the data is fictional.
- Rehearsing only in edit mode and missing slideshow clipping.
- Sharing the full desktop or private speaker notes by mistake.
- Exporting a large video without checking storage or data costs.

## Practice Exercises

1. Turn the supplied outline into ten slide titles before adding content.
2. Choose a theme, modify one master layout and check that all affected slides remain readable.
3. Convert a long paragraph into a headline and a small set of concise bullets.
4. Insert an Excel chart and add a title, units and fictional-data label.
5. Compare the supplied before/after examples and identify three improvements.
6. Add speaker notes to three slides and rehearse a one-minute talk with a timer.
7. Export the deck to PDF and inspect it on a second screen or viewer.
8. Practise sharing one application window in an authorised test meeting or explain the steps without inviting real participants.

## Weekly Assignment (Due Before Week 6)

Submit a **ten-slide pitch deck** for the fictional Fresh Bites catering service. Include a problem, proposed service, sample menu packages, a clearly labelled fictional pricing table, fictional practice-only social-proof sentence, an explicit next step and a contact placeholder. Use a Slide Master or consistent layouts, include speaker notes, and deliver or record a one-minute talk on one slide. Do not present the fictional social-proof line as a real testimonial and do not add unapproved contact information.

**Grading guide:** structure and message 30%, design 30%, use of master and consistency 20%, delivery 20%.

**Definition of done**

- [ ] The deck has ten slides and follows a clear problem-to-next-step story.
- [ ] Layouts, fonts, colours and slide titles are consistent.
- [ ] Figures and the role-play sentence are visibly identified as fictional.
- [ ] Speaker notes are present; the one-minute talk has been rehearsed or recorded.
- [ ] The PDF or deck opens and shows no clipped content.
- [ ] No private notes, unrelated window or unapproved contact detail is shared.
